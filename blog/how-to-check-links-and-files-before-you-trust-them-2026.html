How to Check a Link or File Before You Trust It (2026 Guide)

A friend forwards a link. An email promises a refund if you download a form. A website offers a free version of paid software. Each one takes a single click — and a single click is all a phishing page or a tampered download needs. The good news: you can check almost any link or file in under a minute, for free, right in your browser, before you trust it with your accounts or your computer.

This guide gives you a simple routine: the 10-second look, the free tools that verify a link's real destination, and the file checks that catch tampered downloads. No signup, no software to install.

The 10-second look: read before you click

Most traps fail a careful look. Before clicking any link, hover over it (on a phone, long-press to preview) and read the real address that appears:

  • Check the domain, not the words around it. A link labelled "PayPal Support" that actually points to paypal-support-verify.net is not PayPal. Attackers register lookalike domains with extra words, hyphens and misspellings — the real brand name buried inside a longer domain is a red flag, not proof.
  • Watch for shortened links hiding the destination. A short link tells you nothing about where it goes. Never trust a shortened link from an unknown sender; expand it first with a link preview or an expander tool before deciding.
  • Urgency is a weapon. "Your account will be closed in 2 hours", "Claim your prize now", "Unpaid parcel fee" — pressure is designed to stop you checking. A genuine company rarely threatens instant consequences by email or text, and your bank will never ask you to log in through a link in a message.
  • Attachments deserve the same suspicion. An unexpected invoice, delivery note or "photo" attachment — especially one asking you to enable macros or scripts — should be treated as untrusted until verified.

If anything feels off at this stage, stop. A legitimate sender will not mind you contacting them through their official website or app instead of their link.

Step 2: Check where the site really lives

Sometimes a link looks plausible but you want more evidence. An IP Lookup reveals the server location and network behind a domain — useful when, for example, a supposed local bank's website is hosted in a country it has no connection to, or a "government" page resolves to an ordinary hosting provider. Location alone never proves a site is fake, but a mismatch with what the site claims to be is a strong reason to pause and verify through another channel.

Also check the site's security headers with our Security Headers Checker. Legitimate, well-maintained websites normally set basic protections; a login page missing them entirely is another small signal that the site may not be what it claims. Step 3: Verify a file before you open it

Downloads need the same discipline as links, especially free software, documents from strangers, and anything that arrived as an email attachment:

  • Prefer the official source. Download software only from the developer's own website or your device's official app store. "Free download" mirror sites are one of the most common ways tampered installers spread.
  • Compare the file's hash. Many developers publish a checksum (a long string of letters and numbers) next to a download. After downloading, generate the file's hash with our free Hash Generator and compare the two strings. If they differ by even one character, the file has been changed since the developer published it — delete it and download again from the official source.
  • Check the file type, not just the name. A file called "invoice.pdf.exe" is a program, not a document. Turn on "show file extensions" in your file manager so disguised executables cannot hide behind a familiar icon.
  • Scan before opening. Keep your device's built-in antivirus enabled and let it scan new downloads. On a shared or public computer, be extra careful — our guide on sharing files safely covers the same caution from the sender's side.

Step 4: The "second channel" rule for anything important

For anything involving money, passwords or sensitive information, never trust a single message — verify through a second, independent channel:

  • A bank message asking you to act? Open the bank's official app yourself (not through the link) or call the number on the back of your card.
  • A colleague sharing an "urgent document"? Message them on a different app to confirm they really sent it before opening it.
  • A seller sending a payment link? Find the store through a search or a known address and check the offer there.

This one habit defeats almost every impersonation attack, because attackers can fake a message but they cannot fake the real company's app, phone line or website.

Step 5: If you already clicked or opened it

Do not panic — act quickly and in order:

  1. If you entered a password, change it immediately from the real website (typed by hand, not via the suspicious link), and change the same password anywhere else you reused it.
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication on the affected account. Even a leaked password is far less useful to an attacker once a second factor is required.
  3. If you downloaded something suspicious, do not open it. Delete it, run a full antivirus scan, and if you did run it, consider asking a trusted technician to check the device.
  4. If money is involved, contact your bank at once — the sooner a fraudulent transaction is reported, the better the chance of stopping or reversing it.
  5. Warn the people who might get the same message. If the attack came through a friend's hacked account, tell them through another channel so they can recover it.

The 60-second checklist

  • ☐ Hovered or long-pressed the link and read the real domain
  • ☐ No urgency, threat or too-good-to-be-true promise pushing me to click
  • ☐ Link scanned with a phishing checker before opening
  • ☐ Download came from the official source, and its hash matches if published
  • ☐ File extension shown and matches what the file claims to be
  • ☐ Money or password requests verified through a second channel

Trust on the internet is not about being suspicious of everything — it is about spending sixty seconds verifying the few things that actually matter. Make the checklist a habit and most attacks never get past the first step.

Frequently asked questions

Does a padlock icon mean a link is safe?

No. The padlock only means the connection is encrypted — it says nothing about who runs the site. Phishing pages routinely use encryption too. Always check the domain itself, not just the padlock.

Can a link be dangerous without me downloading anything?

Yes. A phishing page only needs you to type your login details into it, and some malicious pages try to exploit browser weaknesses. That is why checking before you click matters more than scanning after.

Is it safe to paste a suspicious link into a checker tool?

Yes, when the tool analyses the link text rather than opening the page — that is exactly how our phishing checker works. Never paste passwords, private links containing tokens, or personal information into any public tool.

What if a file's hash does not match?

Delete the file and do not run it. A mismatch means the file differs from the developer's published version — it may be corrupted, outdated, or tampered with. Download again from the official source and re-check.
