How to Check and Improve Your Writing's Readability with Free Online Tools (2026 Guide)

Most writing is not rejected because it is wrong. It is abandoned because it is hard to read. A reader opens an email, a product page or a set of instructions, meets a wall of long sentences and heavy words, and quietly leaves. The writer never hears about it. Readability is the fix, and it is not about dumbing your ideas down. It is about removing the friction between your idea and your reader, so the reader spends their energy understanding you instead of decoding you. In this guide, you will learn a simple readability check you can run on anything you write, plus the free browser tools that make each step faster — no signup, no software to install.

What readability really means

Readability is how easily a reader can follow your writing on the first pass. Four things decide it: sentence length, word choice, structure, and layout. Short, clear sentences are easier to follow than long, tangled ones. Plain words beat jargon when the reader is not a specialist. Headings, lists and short paragraphs let a reader scan and find what they need. And a clean layout — no giant blocks of text — invites reading instead of discouraging it.

One caution before we start: readability is a guide, not a grade to chase. A piece can be short and simple and still be unclear, if it leaves out a step or an important detail. Never cut a fact your reader needs just to make a sentence shorter. The goal is writing that is both complete and easy to follow, and the check below keeps both halves in view.

The 5-minute readability check

Run this check on any draft before you send or publish it:

  1. Read it aloud. If you run out of breath in a sentence, it is too long. If you stumble over a word, your reader will too. Reading aloud catches problems your eyes skip, and it takes about a minute for a short piece.
  2. Hunt the long sentences. Look for sentences that run past two lines, or that contain three or more commas and an "and" in the middle. Split each one into two shorter sentences, or turn the middle clause into its own sentence. The meaning stays the same; the effort drops sharply.
  3. Replace jargon with plain words. For every specialist term, ask: would my reader use this word themselves? If not, use the plain word, or explain the term once and then use it consistently. "Use" beats "utilise", "help" beats "facilitate", and "start" beats "commence" — every time, for every reader.
  4. Break up the walls. Any paragraph longer than five or six lines should probably be two paragraphs. Any list buried inside a sentence should become a real list, with one item per line. Readers scan first and read second, so give them something to scan.
  5. Check the opening. The first two sentences decide whether the reader continues. They should say what this piece is about and why it matters to the reader — not background, not apologies, not a long introduction to the introduction.

A before-and-after example

Before: "It is important to note that, in order to facilitate the commencement of the process, users are required to utilise the aforementioned form, which must be completed in full prior to submission, and failure to do so may result in delays."

After: "To start, fill in the form completely before you submit it. An incomplete form causes delays."

The second version says the same thing in a third of the words. It drops "it is important to note", "facilitate", "commence", "utilise" and "aforementioned" — none of which added meaning — and it puts the action first. That is the whole technique: cut the filler, keep the fact, and put the reader's next step where they can find it.

Adapt the check to what you are writing

Emails: One idea per paragraph, the request in the first two lines, and a short list for anything with more than two steps. If the email needs a reply, say exactly what reply you need.

Instructions: Numbered steps, one action per step, and the result of each step stated plainly ("You should now see a confirmation message"). A reader following instructions is busy doing something else; they cannot decode a paragraph and a task at the same time.

Product and service descriptions: Benefits first, facts in a list, and no sentence that exists only to sound impressive. Our guide to product descriptions uses the same principle: replace claims with answers.

School and work notes: Headings for each topic, short paragraphs, and a one-line summary at the top. The summary lets a busy reader decide in seconds whether the rest is for them.

Mistakes that make readability worse

  • Shortening everything: Chopping sentences until they sound like a telegram removes the connections between ideas. Keep the words that link one thought to the next — "because", "so" and "for example" are doing real work.
  • Removing necessary detail: A short instruction that omits a step is not readable, it is broken. Cut words, never facts.
  • Swapping jargon for vagueness: Replacing a precise term with a vague one ("thing", "stuff", "process") does not help anyone. Use the plain word, or explain the precise term once.
  • One format for everything: A list is not always better than a paragraph, and a paragraph is not always better than a list. Use lists for steps, choices and facts; use paragraphs for explanations and reasons.
  • Never re-reading: The single most common readability failure is publishing a first draft. Every draft improves on a second read — and the check above is that second read, done properly.

Frequently asked questions

How long should my sentences be?

There is no magic number, but as a working rule, most sentences in everyday writing should fit on one or two lines. If a sentence needs three lines, check whether it is really two sentences joined together. Variety helps too: a mix of short and medium sentences reads better than every sentence the same length.

Does readability matter for expert readers?

Yes, even more so. Experts are busy, and they skim like everyone else. Clear writing respects an expert's time and makes your argument easier to agree with. Specialists need precise terms, not tangled sentences — give them the terms, in plain sentences.

Can a tool alone make my writing readable?

No. Tools measure length, count characters and clean formatting, and those measurements are genuinely useful. But only a human reader — ideally you, reading aloud — can tell whether a sentence actually makes sense. Use the tools to measure, and your own judgement to decide.

How often should I run this check?

Every time the writing matters: anything you send to a customer, publish online, or submit for marking. For quick messages, the read-aloud step alone is enough. For anything longer, run the full five-minute check — it is the cheapest improvement most writing ever gets.

Readability is not a talent and it is not a trick. It is a short check: read it aloud, split the long sentences, swap jargon for plain words, break up the walls, and measure the result with free tools before you publish. Do that once, and you will feel the difference in your own writing — and so will everyone who reads it. The new one will be shorter, clearer, and easier to finish — which is exactly what your reader wanted.
