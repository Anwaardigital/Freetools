How to Convert PDF to Word Online Free (2026 Guide)

Someone sends you a PDF and asks you to edit it. A contract needs a paragraph changed, a CV needs updating, a form needs your details filled in — and the file is locked inside a PDF. You could retype the whole thing, but that wastes an afternoon and invites typos. Converting the PDF to Word is the sensible fix: you get an editable document you can change, save, and share again. This guide shows you how to do it free in your browser, how to fix the formatting that usually breaks in conversion, and what to do when the PDF is a scanned image instead of real text.

Before you convert: is your PDF ready?

A good conversion starts before you press any button. First, check what kind of PDF you have. Open it and try to select a sentence with your mouse. If the text highlights, your PDF has a real text layer and will convert cleanly. If nothing highlights — the page behaves like a photograph — your PDF is scanned, and you will need the different approach in Step 4. Second, check whether the file is password-protected: if it asks for a password just to open it, you will need that password first, and a locked file cannot be converted until it is unlocked. If the file opens fine but shows a "permissions" warning when you try to copy text, run it through the PDF Unlock tool first to remove the editing restriction. Third, check the file size. A PDF full of high-resolution images converts more slowly and produces a heavy Word file, so if the file is very large, shrink it first with the PDF Compressor. A minute of preparation here saves a lot of cleanup later.

Step 1: Convert the PDF to Word in one click

Open the PDF to Word tool in your browser. The conversion runs entirely on your device, so your document is never uploaded to a server — this matters a lot when the file is a contract, a medical form, or anything with personal details. Pick your PDF file, wait a few seconds while the tool reads the pages and rebuilds them as a Word document, then download the result. The converted file keeps your headings, paragraphs, and most of the layout, and you can open it in Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice, or any app that reads .docx files. For a simple document — a letter, a one-page form, a plain report — this is usually the whole job: convert, open, edit, done. If the document is long, convert it in its original order and check the first and last pages first; most conversion mistakes, if any, show up at page breaks and in headers and footers.

Step 2: Fix the formatting after conversion

Even the best conversion is a reconstruction, not a perfect copy, so expect a few things to need tidying. Headings are the most common issue: a heading that was large and bold in the PDF may come through as ordinary text. Select it, apply the matching heading style in Word, and the document's structure is back. Fonts are next — if the PDF used a font your computer does not have, Word substitutes the nearest one, which can shift line breaks. Pick one clean font for the whole document and apply it consistently rather than fixing lines one by one. Paragraph spacing and line breaks also need a quick pass: remove any double blank lines left over from the PDF layout, and check that paragraphs that were split across pages flow correctly. A fast way to check length and consistency is the Word Counter — paste the text in to see the word count and spot places where repeated spaces or broken lines inflated it. If you pasted headings or labels from different sources, the Case Converter can make their capitalisation consistent in one pass. Ten minutes of cleanup at this stage makes the difference between a document that looks converted and one that looks like it was always a Word file.

Step 3: Rebuild tables and lists

Tables and numbered lists are where conversions struggle most. A table in a PDF is often just text arranged in columns, so the converter has to guess where one cell ends and the next begins — and it sometimes guesses wrong. Open the converted document and check every table row: merged cells, missing borders, and numbers that slipped into the wrong column are the usual problems. The fix is usually quick: select the table in Word, use the table tools to add or delete rows and columns, and re-enter the few values that landed in the wrong place. For numbered lists, watch for numbering that restarts at 1 in the middle of a list or bullet points that became plain dashes — reapply the list style and the numbering repairs itself. If a table is so mangled that fixing it takes longer than rebuilding it, copy the values out as plain text first with the PDF to Text tool, then build a fresh table in Word and paste the values in. That sounds like extra work, but a clean table built in two minutes beats a broken one patched for twenty.

Step 4: What to do with scanned PDFs

A scanned PDF is a set of pictures of pages, not text, so a normal converter has nothing to convert — it will give you back a Word file with images and no editable words. The fix is text recognition, usually called OCR. First, pull the text out of the scanned pages with the Image to Text tool, which reads the words out of the image and gives you plain text you can edit. Work through the document page by page, or at least through the pages you actually need to change, and paste the extracted text into a new Word document. Then rebuild the simple formatting — headings, bold for emphasis, and a basic structure — rather than trying to recreate the original layout exactly. Check the extracted text carefully, because recognition can misread similar-looking characters: the number 0 and the letter O, the number 1 and a lowercase L, and unusual names are the classic mistakes. If only one or two pages of a long scanned PDF need editing, extract just those pages instead of the whole file — you save time and the rest of the document stays untouched as a PDF.

Once your edits are done, you usually want to send the document back as a PDF — it looks the same on every device, and nobody can accidentally change your wording. Use the Word to PDF tool: upload your edited .docx file and download a clean PDF in seconds. If the PDF came out too large to email — images are the usual cause — run it through the PDF Compressor to shrink it. And if your document is now several files — a converted chapter, a scanned appendix, a new cover page — combine them into one file with the PDF Merger, or remove pages you no longer need with the PDF Splitter. The round trip is complete: PDF to Word, edit, Word back to PDF, all without installing anything.

Keep your documents private

Documents you convert are often the most sensitive files you own: contracts, CVs, medical forms, financial records, school certificates. Many online converters upload your file to a server, convert it there, and keep a copy for some period — and their privacy pages rarely say how long. The tools linked in this guide work differently: they run entirely in your browser, so your file never leaves your device. As a habit, avoid converters that ask you to create an account, ask for your email address to send you the result, or add a watermark to your document unless you pay. Also be careful with file names: a converted file often keeps the original name, so rename it before sharing if the original name contains personal details. And when you are done, delete the intermediate files — the original PDF, the converted Word file, and the final PDF — from shared or public computers, keeping only the copies you need.

Common conversion problems and how to fix them

  • Text comes out jumbled or in the wrong order: the PDF likely has multiple columns or text boxes. Convert again after checking the reading order, or use PDF to Text to extract the words and rebuild the layout yourself.
  • Special characters turn into boxes or question marks: the PDF used a font with symbols your system lacks. Replace the broken characters after conversion, and stick to common fonts when you re-save.
  • Images disappear or land in the wrong place: large or floating images confuse the layout. Note where each image belongs, then re-insert it in Word and set its position to sit with the surrounding text.
  • The Word file is much bigger than the PDF: embedded images are usually the cause. Compress the images in the PDF first with the PDF Compressor, then convert again.
  • The file will not convert at all: check that the PDF is not password-locked (unlock it with PDF Unlock), that it is not corrupted (try opening it in another reader), and that it is a text PDF rather than a scan (see Step 4).
  • Page numbers and headers repeat oddly: headers and footers from the PDF often become ordinary text in Word. Delete the stray copies and let Word generate its own header and footer.

Frequently asked questions

Will my formatting survive the conversion?

Mostly. Headings, paragraphs, bold and italic text, and simple tables usually survive well. Complex multi-column layouts, floating images, and unusual fonts need a short cleanup pass afterwards, as described in Steps 2 and 3.

Can I convert a scanned PDF to Word?

Not directly — a scanned PDF contains pictures, not text. Extract the text first with the Image to Text tool, then paste it into Word and rebuild the formatting, as explained in Step 4.

Is my document uploaded anywhere during conversion?

No. The tools in this guide run entirely in your browser, so your file never leaves your device. That makes them safe for contracts, CVs, and other private documents.

What if I only need to change one page?

Convert the whole file, edit the one page, and convert back — or, if the file is long, split out the page you need with the PDF Splitter first, convert just that page, and merge it back with the PDF Merger.
