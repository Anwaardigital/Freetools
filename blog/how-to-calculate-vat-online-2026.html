How to Calculate VAT Online (2026 Guide for Small Businesses and Freelancers)

VAT — value added tax — is the small percentage added to the price of most goods and services in many countries. If you run a small shop, work as a freelancer, or sell anything online, you will meet VAT on almost every invoice, receipt and price quote. A price of 1,000 can become 1,150, or a total of 1,200 can hide 200 of tax inside it — and getting the number wrong means charging too little, quoting too much, or filing the wrong figure at the end of the month. The good news is that VAT maths is simple once you know the two directions it works in: adding VAT to a net price, and removing VAT from a total that already includes it. This guide explains both, with worked examples you can check by hand, the mistakes that catch most beginners, and a free online VAT calculator that does the arithmetic for you in seconds. No app, no signup, no spreadsheet formulas — just the numbers, explained plainly.

What is VAT, in plain words?

VAT is a tax charged on most goods and services, collected little by little at each step of buying and selling. When you buy something for your business, you pay VAT on it. When you sell something to a customer, you charge VAT on it. The customer pays the tax in the end, and the seller passes what they collected to the tax authority, minus the VAT they already paid on their own purchases. That is the whole idea — a tax on the value added at each step, not a tax on the full price again and again.

Rates differ by country, and sometimes by product. Many countries charge one standard rate on most goods and a lower rate on essentials such as food and medicine. Some places do not use VAT at all and use a sales tax or GST instead — the idea is the same, a percentage added to a price, only the name and the rules change. Because rates and rules differ, always check your country’s current rate on your tax authority’s website before you quote a price or file a return. This guide uses 15% and 20% in its examples because they are common round numbers — swap in your own rate and the method is exactly the same.

Two words you will see everywhere in VAT: net and gross. The net price is the price before VAT. The gross price is the price after VAT is added — the total the customer actually pays. Every VAT calculation is just moving between these two numbers.

The two VAT calculations you need

There are only two VAT calculations, and once you can do both, you can handle any invoice, quote or receipt.

Adding VAT: you know the net price and you want the total. Multiply the net price by the rate, divide by 100, and add the result to the net price. In short: VAT amount = net price × rate ÷ 100, and total = net price + VAT amount. For a net price of 1,000 at 15%: the VAT is 1,000 × 15 ÷ 100 = 150, and the total is 1,000 + 150 = 1,150.

Removing VAT: you know the total and you want the net price and the VAT inside it. Divide the total by (1 + rate ÷ 100). In short: net price = total ÷ (1 + rate ÷ 100), and VAT amount = total − net price. For a total of 1,200 at 20%: the net price is 1,200 ÷ 1.20 = 1,000, and the VAT is 1,200 − 1,000 = 200.

Notice the trap: you cannot remove VAT by simply subtracting 20% from the total. Twenty percent of 1,200 is 240, which would give you 960 — the wrong answer. The 20% was calculated on the net price of 1,000, not on the total of 1,200. This one mistake is behind most wrong VAT figures, and it is the reason the division formula above matters. If you remember only one thing from this guide, remember this: to remove VAT, divide — never subtract the percentage from the total.

Worked examples you can check by hand

Try these with a pen or with the free VAT Calculator, and check that your answers match.

Example 1 — add 15% VAT to 1,000. The VAT is 1,000 × 15 ÷ 100 = 150. The total is 1,000 + 150 = 1,150. This is the most common VAT calculation: a freelancer quoting a job at a net price of 1,000 adds 150 of VAT and invoices the client 1,150.

Example 2 — add 20% VAT to 850. The VAT is 850 × 20 ÷ 100 = 170. The total is 850 + 170 = 1,020. A shop selling an item for 850 before tax would put 1,020 on the price tag.

Example 3 — remove 20% VAT from 1,200. The net price is 1,200 ÷ 1.20 = 1,000. The VAT inside the total is 1,200 − 1,000 = 200. You see a receipt for 1,200 and you want to know how much of it was tax: 200.

Example 4 — remove 15% VAT from 2,300. The net price is 2,300 ÷ 1.15 = 2,000. The VAT is 2,300 − 2,000 = 300. A clean round result — and a good way to test yourself, because a wrong method gives a messy number here.

Example 5 — find the VAT amount from a total alone. A customer paid 575 and the rate is 15%. First find the net price: 575 ÷ 1.15 = 500. Then the VAT: 575 − 500 = 75. You now know the sale was worth 500 before tax and the tax was 75 — the two numbers your records need.

Work each example once by hand, even if you plan to use a calculator every time after that. Knowing what the right answer looks like means you will spot a wrong answer instantly, instead of copying a typo into an invoice.

VAT on invoices: what freelancers should show

If you freelance — writing, design, repairs, tutoring, deliveries — your invoices need to show VAT clearly, or your client cannot claim it back and your own records become a mess. A clean invoice has three numbers on separate lines: the net amount for the work, the VAT amount, and the total. For example: “Design work — 1,000; VAT at 15% — 150; Total due — 1,150.” Nothing is hidden, nothing has to be guessed, and both sides can file the same figures.

If the numbers are already separate, the return is an afternoon’s work. If they are mixed together in one pile of totals, it is a week of untangling.

VAT for shop owners: discounts, totals and the till

Shops meet VAT in a slightly different way, because prices change all the time — sales, discounts, bundles and refunds all move the numbers. The rule stays the same: VAT is calculated on the price the customer actually pays, after any discount.

Take an item priced at 1,000 with a 10% discount and 15% VAT. First apply the discount: 1,000 − 100 = 900. Then add the VAT: 900 × 15 ÷ 100 = 135. The customer pays 900 + 135 = 1,035. If you added the VAT first and then discounted, you would get 1,150 − 115 = 1,035 — the same total, because both are percentages of the same base. What matters is not the order, but that the discount and the VAT are both applied once, to the right base, and that the receipt shows them separately.

VAT across currencies

If you buy from one country and sell in another, VAT meets the exchange rate — and the order you do the maths in can confuse people. The reassuring fact is that multiplication does not care about order: converting first and then adding VAT gives the same result as adding VAT first and then converting, as long as you use the same exchange rate for both steps.

Say you buy an item for 100 dollars, your currency converts at 280 per dollar, and your VAT rate is 15%. Convert first: 100 × 280 = 28,000, then add VAT: 28,000 × 1.15 = 32,200. Or add VAT first: 100 × 1.15 = 115 dollars, then convert: 115 × 280 = 32,200. Same answer. Use the free Currency Converter for the exchange step and the VAT Calculator for the tax step, and keep the two steps separate so you can see where each number came from.

Watch out for one real-world catch: banks and payment services add their own fee or use a slightly different rate than the one you see online. That difference is a cost, not a tax — it does not change the VAT, but it does change your profit. Record the rate you actually paid, not the rate you looked up, when you work out what a sale really earned you.

Six mistakes that ruin VAT figures

Most wrong VAT numbers come from the same few mistakes. Learn them once and you will avoid them for years.

1. Subtracting the rate from the total. Removing 20% VAT from 1,200 by subtracting 240 gives 960 — wrong. Divide by 1.20 and you get the true net price of 1,000. This is the most common VAT mistake in the world.

2. Adding VAT twice. Taking a price that already includes VAT and adding VAT again. If the price tag says 1,150 and VAT is already inside it, adding another 15% charges the customer 1,322.50 for a 1,000 item. Always ask: is this price net or gross?

3. Using the wrong rate. Rates change, and some goods use a reduced rate. Quoting with last year’s rate, or the standard rate on a reduced-rate item, makes every figure on the invoice wrong. Check the current rate before a big quote, not after the client has paid.

4. Rounding too early. Rounding each line of an invoice and then adding the rounded lines can leave the total a few units out. Do all the maths with full numbers, and round only the final total — the way the VAT Calculator does it.

5. Forgetting VAT on delivery and fees. In many places, delivery charges and service fees carry VAT too. If you add delivery to an order after calculating VAT on the goods alone, the tax on the delivery is missing. Add everything the customer pays for first, then calculate the tax.

6. Mixing currencies. Calculating VAT in one currency on a price quoted in another, without converting first. The percentage is the same, but the money is not — a 15% VAT on 100 dollars is not 15 in your local currency. Convert, then calculate, and keep the currency on every figure you write down.

A simple monthly VAT routine

VAT stops being stressful when it becomes a routine instead of a scramble. Here is a short one you can run at the end of every month:

  1. Gather your sales invoices: add up the VAT you charged on everything you sold. The VAT Calculator can re-check any figure you are unsure about.
  2. Gather your purchase receipts: add up the VAT you paid on things you bought for the business.
  3. Find the difference: VAT charged minus VAT paid is roughly what you owe — or what you can claim back, if you paid more than you charged.
  4. Check three invoices by hand: pick three at random and re-do the maths with the formulas in this guide. If all three match, your records are probably right.
  5. File the numbers the way your tax authority asks: most want the net sales, the VAT charged, the net purchases and the VAT paid as four separate figures — which is why this guide keeps them separate from the start.
  6. Save everything: invoices, receipts and your monthly totals, in one folder, for at least as long as your country’s rules require.

Thirty minutes a month, every month, beats a panicked week at the end of the year. The routine is the same whether you sell ten items or ten thousand — only the pile of paper changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between VAT and sales tax?

Both are taxes added to a price as a percentage, but they are collected differently. VAT is collected in small steps at every stage of production and sale, with each business claiming back the VAT it paid. Sales tax is usually charged only once, at the final sale to the customer. The maths you use — adding or removing a percentage — is the same for both.

How do I remove VAT from a price?

Divide the total by (1 + rate ÷ 100). For a total of 1,200 at 20% VAT, divide 1,200 by 1.20 to get a net price of 1,000, and the VAT is 200. Never subtract the percentage from the total — that gives the wrong answer.

How do I calculate VAT on a normal calculator?

To add VAT, multiply the net price by the rate, divide by 100, and add the result to the net price. For 1,000 at 15%: 1,000 × 15 ÷ 100 = 150, and 1,000 + 150 = 1,150. To remove VAT, divide the total by 1 plus the rate as a decimal — 1,200 ÷ 1.20 = 1,000 at 20%.

What if my country has no VAT?

Then it probably has a sales tax, a GST, or no consumption tax at all. The method in this guide still works for any percentage tax: the formulas for adding and removing a percentage from a price do not depend on what the tax is called. Check your local rules for the right rate and the right name to put on your invoices.

Should VAT be shown separately on an invoice?

Yes, in almost every country that charges VAT. Show the net amount, the VAT amount with the rate, and the total as three separate lines. It lets your customer claim the VAT back, keeps your records clean, and is usually a legal requirement for business-to-business invoices.

How do I check my VAT maths quickly?

Work backwards. If you added VAT, subtract it again with the division formula and confirm you get back to your starting price. If the numbers do not match, one of the two steps is wrong.
