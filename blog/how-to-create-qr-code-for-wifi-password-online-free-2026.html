How to Create a QR Code for Your Wi-Fi Password Online Free (2026 Guide)

A guest arrives at your home, your shop, or your office, and the first thing they ask is: "What is the Wi-Fi password?" You spell out a long password full of capital letters, numbers and symbols. They type it wrong twice, you spell it again, and five minutes later they are still not connected. There is a much simpler way: a Wi-Fi QR code. Print it once, stick it on the wall or the counter, and any guest joins your network by pointing their phone camera at it — no typing, no spelling, no mistakes. This guide shows you how to create a QR code for your Wi-Fi password online for free, in under a minute, with no app to install and no account to create — plus how guests scan it on iPhone and Android, how to print it so it scans first time, and the security rules that keep your network safe while you share it.

What is a Wi-Fi QR code?

A Wi-Fi QR code is a normal QR code that holds your network name, password and security type inside it, instead of a web link. When a phone scans it, the phone reads those details and offers to join the network with one tap. The guest never sees your password and never types anything — the phone does the whole job. The code follows a simple standard format that every modern phone understands: it stores the network name (called the SSID), the password, and the encryption type, usually WPA or WPA2. Because the information is stored directly in the pattern of black squares, the code works forever and needs no internet connection to be scanned — it only needs the phone to be near your router when it connects, like any Wi-Fi login.

Wi-Fi QR codes are now everywhere for a good reason. Cafes print them on table cards, hotels put them in the room, offices stick them near the reception desk, and families keep one on the fridge. They turn a small daily annoyance into a one-second action, and they work for everyone — even guests who are not comfortable with technology, because scanning a code with a camera is easier than typing a sixteen-character password correctly.

Why use a Wi-Fi QR code instead of sharing the password?

  • No typing mistakes: Long passwords with mixed capitals, numbers and symbols are easy to mistype, especially on a small phone keyboard. A scan removes typing completely, so the first try works.
  • Faster for everyone: A guest connects in about five seconds. In a busy cafe or shop, that means no queue at the counter just to ask for the password.
  • Your password stays private: The guest's phone reads the password from the code, but the guest never has to see it or remember it. That matters when your password is also used elsewhere, or when you simply do not want it written on a whiteboard for the whole room to read.
  • Works for every guest: iPhone, Android, tablet or laptop with a camera — one printed code serves them all. You print it once and it keeps working for every visitor.
  • Looks professional: A neat printed card with a QR code looks far better in a shop, clinic, classroom or office than a hand-written password taped to the wall.
  • Easy to update: Change your Wi-Fi password and you only need to print a new code. The old one simply stops working, which is exactly what you want.

How guests scan the code on iPhone and Android

Guests do not need a special app. On almost every phone made in the last few years, the built-in camera does the job:

On iPhone

Open the Camera app and point it at the code. A yellow banner appears at the top of the screen saying "Wi-Fi QR Code" with the network name. Tap the banner, then tap Join, and the phone connects. This works on iPhones running iOS 11 or later — which covers every iPhone sold in the last eight years. If the banner does not appear, make sure the camera is steady and the whole code is in the frame, and check in Settings, then Camera, that Scan QR Codes is turned on.

On Android

Open the Camera app and point it at the code. Most Android phones show a pop-up offering to connect to the network — tap it and confirm. On some phones the camera does not scan QR codes by itself; in that case, open the phone's Settings, go to Wi-Fi, tap the QR code icon (usually at the top right), and scan the code from there. Android 10 and later supports Wi-Fi QR codes in the system itself, so no extra app is needed.

On older phones or laptops

Older phones without built-in QR scanning can use any free QR scanner app from their app store. On a laptop, open the phone's camera or a webcam QR reader — or simply keep the password written down somewhere safe as a backup for guests whose devices cannot scan. A Wi-Fi QR code is a convenience, not a replacement for knowing your own password.

Printing tips: make your code scan first time

A QR code only works if the camera can read it clearly. These rules make the difference between a code that scans instantly and one that guests keep struggling with:

  • Print at least 2 × 2 cm: That is the smallest size that scans reliably. For a wall sign or a table card that guests scan from a step away, print it larger — 5 cm or more is comfortable.
  • Keep the white margin: The white border around the code, called the quiet zone, is part of the code. Never crop it off and never let text or a logo touch the squares.
  • Black on white only: The strongest contrast scans best. Fancy coloured codes, white squares on a dark background, or a logo placed over the middle can all make the code unreadable.
  • Use the high-resolution PNG: Download the PNG from the generator instead of screenshotting it. A screenshot is low resolution and prints blurry, and blurry squares do not scan.
  • Print on matt paper: Glossy paper reflects light and camera flash, which can wash out the squares. Plain matt paper scans far more reliably, especially under bright shop lights.
  • Add the network name underneath: Print the network name below the code, so guests can check they are joining the right network — and so you have a fallback if the code ever gets damaged.
  • Test the printed copy: Scan the printed card with two different phones before you put it out. A code that works on screen can fail in print if the printer made it too small or too faint.

What to do when your Wi-Fi password changes

Changing your Wi-Fi password is good practice, but it breaks every old QR code instantly — the old code still holds the old password, so guests scanning it will get a "wrong password" error. Whenever you change the password, make a new QR code the same day and replace every printed copy: the fridge card, the shop counter, the meeting room sign. Throw the old cards away, or guests will keep scanning a code that no longer works. This is also why you should never print the password itself on the card: if the password changes, the card is useless anyway, and if the card is stolen or photographed, the password goes with it.

One more thing to plan for: after a password change, devices that connected before will ask for the new password once. That is normal. Guests who scan the new code connect straight away, and your own devices only need the new password typed in once.

Security rules: share Wi-Fi safely

A Wi-Fi QR code makes sharing easy, which is exactly why you should think about who you are sharing with. A printed code in a public place is, in practice, an open invitation to your network — anyone who can see it can join. Follow these rules:

  • Use a guest network for visitors: Most modern routers let you create a separate guest network with its own name and password. Guests get internet, but they cannot see your computers, printers, shared files or smart-home devices. Make the QR code for the guest network, never for your main network, in a shop, office or any place strangers visit.
  • Keep a strong Wi-Fi password: A QR code does not make a weak password safe. Use a long password — at least 12 characters with a mix of letters, numbers and symbols. If you need a new one, the Password Generator creates one in a second, and the Password Strength Checker tells you honestly how strong it is.
  • Do not print the password on the card: The code already carries the password inside it. Printing the password in plain text underneath defeats the whole point and lets anyone photograph it from across the room.
  • Keep the code where it belongs: A code on your living-room wall is fine for family friends. A code in a shop window can be scanned by anyone walking past, including people you never invited. Place it where only your real guests can reach it.
  • Change the guest password regularly: In a business, change the guest network password every few months and print a fresh code. Old codes stop working automatically, which quietly removes everyone who had access before.
  • Turn off WPS: WPS is an older one-button way to join Wi-Fi that has known security weaknesses. If your router has it, turn it off in the router settings and use the QR code instead.

Frequently asked questions

Yes. The QR Code Generator is completely free, with no signup, no limits and no watermark. You can make as many codes as you like, for home, work or business.

Does the QR code expire?

No. The code stores your network details directly, so it works forever — until you change the Wi-Fi password or the network name. There is no server and no expiry date involved.

Will it work on iPhone and Android?

Yes. iPhones running iOS 11 or later scan Wi-Fi QR codes with the built-in Camera app, and Android 10 and later does the same, either with the camera or from the Wi-Fi settings screen. Older phones can use any free QR scanner app.

Can someone steal my password from the QR code?

Anyone who scans the code with a QR reader can see the password inside it, because the code has to contain the password to work. That is why you should use a guest network for visitors and never place a code for your main network where strangers can photograph it.

What if the code does not scan?

Check three things: the network name and password are typed exactly right (Wi-Fi names are case-sensitive), the printed code is at least 2 × 2 cm with its white margin intact, and the whole code is inside the camera frame in good light. If it still fails, generate a fresh code and test it on screen before printing again.

Does the QR generator see or store my Wi-Fi password?
