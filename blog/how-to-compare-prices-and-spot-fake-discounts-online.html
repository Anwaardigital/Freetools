How to Compare Prices and Spot Fake Discounts Online (2026 Guide)

"50% OFF!" "MEGA SALE — ENDS TONIGHT!" Online stores are experts at making every price feel like a bargain. Some sales are genuine; many are carefully engineered illusions. The difference between a smart shopper and an overcharged one usually comes down to a little arithmetic — and arithmetic is exactly what browsers do best. This guide teaches you the price-comparison skills that protect your wallet, with free tools that do the math instantly and privately.

Step 1: Always compare the unit price, not the sticker price

Two bottles of olive oil: 500 ml for $4.99 vs. 750 ml for $6.49. Which is cheaper? Your eyes see the smaller total and say $4.99 — but the answer is price per unit. Divide price by quantity: $4.99 ÷ 500 = $0.00998 per ml, vs. $6.49 ÷ 750 = $0.00865 per ml. The bigger bottle is the better deal. This one habit — always reducing to a per-unit price — instantly beats most marketing tricks.

The catch: comparing different units (grams vs. ounces, liters vs. gallons, yards vs. meters) is tedious by hand. Use our free Unit Converter to put everything in the same unit first, then do the division. Many grocery sites and supermarket shelf labels now show unit prices in small print — but when they don't, the converter plus two seconds of division beats guesswork every time.

Step 2: Learn real discount math in 30 seconds

A "30% off" sign tells you nothing until you know the final price — and stores sometimes get the arithmetic wrong on purpose. The formula is simple: final price = original × (1 − discount ÷ 100). A $80 jacket at 30% off costs $80 × 0.70 = $56.

Run the numbers before you feel excited about the badge; the math doesn't care about the red font.

Step 3: Spot the fake-discount playbook

Not every sale is a scam, but some common tactics are designed to fool you. Learn to recognize them:

  • The inflated "was" price: "Was $199, now $99!" — but was it ever $199? Check the seller's price history with a tracking tool or search the product name with recent dates. A discount against a price that never existed is a lie, not a deal.
  • Perpetual sales: A store whose sale "ends Sunday" every single Sunday isn't having a sale — the sale price is the real price. Compare it against other sellers instead of the store's own "original" price.
  • Shrinkflation disguised as a deal: The price dropped, but so did the package size. This is where unit prices (Step 1) expose the trick immediately.
  • Doorbuster bait: A jaw-dropping price on one item, designed to get you on the site so you buy full-price accessories. The headline item may be genuinely cheap — but budget for the full basket, not the headline.
  • Fake urgency: Countdown timers that reset, "only 2 left!" on digital goods, popups claiming someone in your city just bought it. Real scarcity doesn't need a flashing timer. If the pressure feels artificial, close the tab and sleep on it.
  • Coupon games: "Extra 20% off with code!" that brings the price exactly back to what competitors charge every day. The code isn't a gift — it's a pricing dance. Compare the final checkout total, not the headline.

Step 4: Compare across sellers — and currencies

The same product often sells at wildly different prices across marketplaces, brand sites, and local stores. Check at least three sellers, and compare the total landed price: item + shipping + taxes + any import fees. A "$10 cheaper" listing with $15 shipping is more expensive.

Shopping from international sites? Currency conversions add a hidden margin: your card issuer's exchange rate and foreign-transaction fees can turn a bargain into a wash. If the margin is thin after a realistic conversion, buy local.

Step 5: Splitting costs? Do the math once, not three times

Group purchases — gifts, shared household items, restaurant bills after the "deal" dinner — create their own arguments. Decide shares before money moves. A 60-second pre-purchase checklist

  1. Compute the unit price and compare like-for-like quantities (Unit Converter).
  2. Run the discount math yourself — don't trust the badge (Discount Calculator, Percentage Calculator).
  3. Verify the "was" price — is the sale real or perpetual?
  4. Compare total landed price across at least 3 sellers, including shipping and taxes.
  5. Converting currencies? Check the real rate (Currency Converter).
  6. Sleep on anything over your impulse-buy threshold. Real deals survive the night; fake urgency doesn't.

Frequently asked questions

Are online "was" prices regulated?

Rules vary by country, and enforcement is patchy. Treat every "was" price as a marketing claim until you verify it against the seller's own price history or competing sellers.

What's the quickest way to compare two pack sizes?

Divide each price by its quantity (converting units first with the Unit Converter if needed) and compare the per-unit results. Lower per-unit price wins — assuming you'll actually use the larger pack before it expires.

Do coupon codes ever make a real difference?

Sometimes — especially stackable codes on already-discounted items, or first-order codes. Always apply the code and compare the final checkout total against other sellers; that's the only number that matters.

Is it worth converting currency manually for small purchases?

For small amounts, a rough conversion is fine. For bigger purchases, a 2–3% bank margin on a large price can erase the whole discount — run the Currency Converter and add your card's foreign-transaction fee to be sure.
