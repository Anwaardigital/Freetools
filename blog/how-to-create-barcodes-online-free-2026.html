How to Create Barcodes Online for Free (2026 Guide)

Barcodes are the quiet workhorses of everyday business. Every product on a shop shelf, every book in a library, every parcel in transit carries one — a pattern of bars that lets a scanner identify an item in a fraction of a second, without typing and without mistakes. You do not need expensive software or a paid subscription to make them. A free browser tool can generate a proper, scannable barcode in under a minute. This guide explains which barcode type you need, how to create one step by step, and how to print it so it scans reliably the first time.

What is a barcode, exactly?

A barcode is a machine-readable way of writing a number. The familiar striped pattern encodes digits (and sometimes letters) as bars of different widths, plus a check digit that lets the scanner catch misreads. A scanner reads the pattern, converts it back to the number, and looks that number up in a database — a shop's price list, a library catalogue, or an inventory system. The barcode itself contains no product information beyond the number; all the meaning lives in the system that reads it.

That is why barcodes are so useful for small businesses, home sellers and organizers: you control the numbers. Assign each product, book or box its own number, print a barcode for it, and any phone or scanner can identify it instantly. No typing, no mix-ups, no searching through spreadsheets by hand.

Which barcode type do you need?

There are dozens of barcode formats, but almost everyone needs one of these four:

  • Code 128: The best default choice. It encodes numbers, letters and symbols, stays compact, and is read by virtually every scanner and phone app. Use it for internal inventory, asset labels, file boxes, event tickets and warehouse stock.
  • EAN-13: The standard retail barcode printed on products sold in shops. It holds exactly 13 digits and is what checkout scanners expect. Use it only if you are labelling products for retail sale — and note that a genuine retail EAN number must come from the official numbering authority for your country, not be invented.
  • UPC-A: The North American retail equivalent, with 12 digits. It is mainly relevant if you sell products in the United States or Canada.
  • Code 39: An older, simpler format still common in libraries, manufacturing and some logistics systems. It is wider than Code 128 for the same data, but its simplicity makes it a safe choice when an older scanner is involved.

Rule of thumb: if the barcode is only for your own tracking — stock, books, equipment, storage boxes — use Code 128. If it is for products going through a retail checkout, find out which format your retailer or marketplace requires before printing anything.

Barcode vs. QR code: which one should you use?

Barcodes and QR codes solve different problems, and picking the wrong one is the most common beginner mistake. A barcode holds a short number and is read by laser scanners at checkouts and in warehouses — it is the right choice when a scanner or a simple inventory app does the reading. A QR code holds much more data — a website address, WiFi details, a contact card — and is read by phone cameras. If a customer should scan it with their phone to reach a website or menu, you want a QR Code Generator instead. If a scanner or your own stock system should identify an item, a barcode is correct. Many businesses use both: a barcode on the product for checkout, and a QR code on the packaging linking to instructions or a website.

Printing barcodes that actually scan

A barcode that looks fine on screen can still fail at the scanner. These printing rules prevent almost every failure:

  • Size matters: Print barcodes at least 3 cm wide for Code 128 and retail formats. Smaller codes force scanners to work harder and fail more often, especially in poor light.
  • Keep contrast high: Black bars on white background scan most reliably. Coloured bars, patterned backgrounds and transparent labels all increase failure rates — save the design flair for the rest of the label.
  • Leave a quiet zone: Barcodes need blank space on both sides (at least a few millimetres) so the scanner can tell where the code starts and ends. Never let text, borders or logos crowd the bars.
  • Never stretch or squash: Resizing a barcode image unevenly changes the bar widths and breaks the encoding. Scale it proportionally, or regenerate it at the size you need.
  • Print at good quality: A blurry or low-resolution print blurs bar edges together. Use a laser printer or a label printer, and avoid printing barcodes on glossy or curved surfaces where glare interferes.
  • Always test before bulk printing: Print one copy and scan it with a phone barcode app and, if you have one, a real scanner. If it reads instantly on both, the batch will be fine. This one test saves entire wasted print runs.

Practical uses for free barcodes

  • Small shop stock: Label every product with a Code 128 barcode and track sales and stock levels with a phone scanner app — no expensive point-of-sale system needed.
  • Home library: Number your books, stick a barcode inside each cover, and keep a simple spreadsheet of what you own and who borrowed what.
  • Storage and moving boxes: One barcode per box, linked to a list of contents. Scan the box to see what is inside without opening it.
  • Event tickets and check-ins: A unique barcode per ticket, scanned at the door — faster than checking names off a list.
  • Equipment and tools: Label workshop tools, office equipment or school supplies so loans and returns can be logged by scan.
  • Market stall and craft sellers: Price labels with barcodes make checkout faster and stock-taking far less painful at the end of the day.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Inventing a retail EAN number: Retail barcodes are registered numbers. Making one up can collide with another real product's number and cause checkout chaos. For retail, get numbers through the proper channel; for internal use, any Code 128 number is fine.
  • Reusing numbers: Every item type needs its own number. Reusing one number for two products makes your stock records meaningless.
  • Printing too small or on shiny labels: The two most common reasons a barcode will not scan, and both are avoidable with the printing rules above.
  • Skipping the test scan: Never print a hundred labels before scanning one. The test takes ten seconds and catches every formatting mistake.
  • Putting barcodes where they get damaged: Avoid folds, seams, edges and places that rub. A barcode on the bottom of a box survives longer than one on a handle.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to register a barcode for internal use?

No. Registration only matters for retail barcodes (EAN/UPC) that pass through other companies' checkout systems. For your own stock, library or storage tracking, any Code 128 number you choose works.

Can a phone scan a barcode?

Yes — free barcode scanner apps read Code 128, EAN and most other formats using the phone camera. For high-volume scanning, a dedicated handheld scanner is faster, but a phone is plenty for small businesses.

Can I put a barcode and a QR code on the same label?

Yes, and it is often smart: the barcode for checkout and stock systems, the QR code for customers who want product details, instructions or your website.

Do barcodes expire?

No. A printed barcode stays valid as long as it stays readable. Only your own records can make a number obsolete — if you retire a product, retire its number too.
