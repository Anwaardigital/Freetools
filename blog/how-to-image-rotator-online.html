How to Rotate and Flip Images Online for Free (2026 Guide)

You take a great photo, send it to a friend — and it arrives sideways. Or you download a picture that was saved upside down, and now every viewer you open shows it the wrong way. Fixing this is one of the simplest image edits there is: rotate the pixels, save, done. This guide shows you exactly how to do it with our free Image Rotator — no software to install, no account to create, and your photos never leave your device.

What the free image rotator does

The Image Rotator runs entirely in your web browser and offers four transformations:

  • Rotate 90° — turns a portrait photo into landscape (or landscape into portrait).
  • Rotate 180° — flips an upside-down image right-side up.
  • Flip horizontally — mirrors the image left-to-right, like a reflection in a mirror.
  • Flip vertically — mirrors the image top-to-bottom.

You can combine them — for example, rotate 90° and then flip horizontally — and preview the result before downloading.

Rotate vs. flip: what's the difference?

Rotation and flipping are easy to confuse, but they do very different things. Rotation spins the image around its center: 90° turns a vertical photo horizontal, and 180° turns an upside-down photo right-side up. The image keeps the same left-right order of its contents.

Flipping mirrors the image. Flip horizontally and the left side becomes the right side — text in the image will appear backwards, exactly like a mirror reflection. Flip vertically and the top becomes the bottom. Flipping never changes orientation (portrait stays portrait); it only reverses one axis.

The key test: if text or faces in your photo look "correct" but the whole thing is tilted, you need rotation. If everything is readable but appears backwards — like a selfie where text on a shirt reads the wrong way — you need a horizontal flip.

Why do phone photos end up sideways?

This is the most common reason people search for an image rotator, and it has a technical cause worth understanding. When you take a photo with your phone held sideways, the camera sensor actually captures the image in one fixed orientation and stores a small piece of metadata called the EXIF orientation tag that says "rotate me 90° when displaying."

Your phone's gallery app reads that tag and shows the photo correctly. But many other programs — some desktop viewers, older email clients, and certain websites — ignore the tag and show the raw sensor orientation instead. The result: the photo looks fine on your phone but arrives sideways for everyone else.

Rotating the image with the tool fixes this permanently. The rotation is applied to the actual pixels and the file is saved in the corrected orientation, so it displays correctly even in apps that ignore the EXIF tag.

Common fixes and which button to use

  • Photo is sideways (portrait shown as landscape): rotate 90°. If it ends up sideways the other way, rotate 90° twice more — three 90° turns equal a 270° turn.
  • Photo is upside down: rotate 180° once.
  • Selfie looks mirrored (text on clothing reads backwards): flip horizontally. Front cameras often save a mirrored preview; flipping it back makes the photo match how other people actually see you.
  • Scanned document is upside down: rotate 180°.
  • Image needs to face the other direction (e.g. an arrow pointing left instead of right): flip horizontally.

Tips for the best results

  • Work from the original file when you can. Every image editor re-encodes the file when it saves. Starting from the highest-quality original — the photo as it came off your camera — gives you the cleanest final result.
  • Rotate before you compress or resize. If you plan to also shrink the image or reduce its file size, do the rotation first, then use our Image Compressor or Image Resizer. That way the final export is produced in one clean step from the correctly oriented image.
  • Check text in the preview. If your image contains text, signs, or logos, zoom into the preview and confirm the text reads correctly before downloading. Mirrored text is the easiest mistake to miss.
  • Keep a copy of the original. The download is a new file, so your original stays untouched on your device — but it's still good practice to keep originals in case you ever want to undo an edit.
