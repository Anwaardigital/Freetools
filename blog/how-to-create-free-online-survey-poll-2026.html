How to Create a Free Online Survey or Poll (2026 Guide)

Want to know what your customers really think, which day suits your class, or whether your friends prefer pizza or biryani at the party? Guessing is slow and often wrong. A survey or a poll gives you the real answer in a few hours, straight from the people who matter. And you do not need to pay for it or install anything. Free online form tools can build a clean survey in minutes, and a simple link or QR code is all you need to share it. This guide shows you the whole process: when to use a survey and when a quick poll is enough, how to write questions people can actually answer, which free tools to use, how to share your survey so people actually open it, and how to read the results without fooling yourself. By the end, you will have a survey you can send today — and answers you can trust.

Survey or poll — which one do you need?

A poll asks one quick question with a few fixed answers: “Which day should we meet — Friday or Saturday?” People answer in two seconds, and you get a clear winner. Use a poll when you need one fast decision and the options are already clear.

A survey asks several questions and digs deeper: why customers leave, what students find hard, how happy your team is. It takes longer to answer and longer to build, but it tells you the reasons behind the numbers, not just the numbers.

A simple rule: if one question settles it, run a poll. If you need reasons, opinions, or details, run a survey. Many people start with a poll to pick a direction, then follow up with a short survey to understand the why. Both are free to make, and both work the same way: a link, a few questions, and honest answers.

Start with one clear goal

Every good survey starts before the first question, with one sentence that says what you want to learn. “Find out why customers stop ordering after the first month.” “Find out which class time students prefer.” “Find out what our readers want us to write about next.” Write that sentence down before you build anything, and check every question against it: does this question help answer that sentence? If it does not, delete it. A survey without a goal collects answers nobody uses. It also annoys the people answering it, because they can feel the questions going nowhere. One goal also keeps you honest later, when you read the results. You will know exactly what the survey was for, so you will not twist a random answer into a decision it was never meant to support.

Write questions people can actually answer

The quality of your answers is decided by the quality of your questions. A good question is short, specific, and neutral — it does not push the person toward the answer you want.

Ask one thing at a time. “Was the food good and was the delivery fast?” is two questions wearing one costume. Some people loved the food and hated the delivery, and they have no honest way to answer. Split it: “How was the food?” and “How fast was the delivery?”

Use plain words. “How satisfied are you with our service?” is fine. Long, formal wording is not — most people will guess, skip, or quit. If a question needs a dictionary, rewrite it.

Never lead the person answering. “Don’t you agree our new design is much better?” is not a question, it is a compliment looking for agreement. Ask instead: “What do you think of the new design?” You might hear something you did not want to hear, and that is exactly the point.

Give people a way out. Add “Not sure” or “Does not apply” where it fits. Forcing an answer from someone who has no opinion only adds noise to your results.

Test your questions before you send the survey. Read each one out loud, or send the survey to two friends first. If anyone pauses, frowns, or asks “what does this mean?”, rewrite that question. A five-minute test saves you from a week of useless answers.

Pick the right question type

Each type of question does a different job. Use the right one and your survey becomes easier to answer and easier to read.

Multiple choice is the workhorse. Use it when the answers are known: “Which flavour do you like best?” with four or five options. Keep the list short — more than six options and people stop reading.

Yes or no works for simple facts: “Did you find what you were looking for?” Use it sparingly. A survey full of yes-or-no questions feels like an interrogation, not a conversation.

Rating scales measure feelings: “How easy was it to order?” from 1 to 5, or from “very hard” to “very easy”. Use the same scale through the whole survey so people do not have to relearn it on every question.

Open text boxes are where the gold is. “What should we improve?” lets people say things you never thought to ask about. But open questions take effort, so use only one or two in a survey, and put them near the end, after the easy questions have warmed the person up.

Ranking questions (“put these in order”) are powerful but tiring. Use them only when the order truly matters, and never rank more than five items.

The best surveys mix types: a few quick multiple-choice questions, one or two ratings, and one open question at the end. That mix keeps people moving and still gives you real insight.

How long should your survey be?

Shorter than you think. Most people will happily answer five questions. Ten questions is fine if the topic matters to them. Twenty questions is where most people quit — and the people who quit are not random, which quietly skews your results.

A good target is five to ten questions that take under three minutes. Say the time honestly at the start: “Three minutes, eight questions.” People decide in seconds whether to begin, and an honest time estimate is the best invitation you can give.

Cut ruthlessly. Every question must earn its place. “Nice to know” is not a reason to keep a question. Ask yourself: what decision will this answer change? If the answer is “none”, delete the question. Your response rate will thank you.

Build it free with online tools

You do not need paid software to make a professional survey. Several well-known tools offer free plans that cover everything a small survey needs.

Google Forms is the most popular free option. It is free with a Google account, offers all the question types above, collects answers into a simple summary with charts, and lets you send the results to a spreadsheet for a closer look.

Microsoft Forms is a similar free choice if you use a Microsoft account, with clean templates and the same kind of automatic charts.

Typeform offers a free plan with a different style: one question per screen, which feels more like a conversation and often gets better completion rates on phones.

For a one-question poll, a tool like StrawPoll is even simpler: write the question, add the options, share the link, and watch the votes come in live.

Pick one tool and learn it properly rather than trying all four. They all do the same job: a link you can share, answers collected in one place, and a summary you can read. The quality of the survey comes from your questions, not from the tool.

Make it look clean and trustworthy

People answer surveys from strangers carefully, and from people they trust openly. A few small touches make your survey look like it comes from a real person, not a spam trap.

Give it a clear title that says what it is about: “Help us improve our bakery — 3-minute survey”, not “Survey 1”. Add one short line at the top explaining why you are asking and what you will do with the answers. People answer more honestly when they know the purpose.

Keep the design simple. One question per screen on phones, clear buttons, and no walls of text. If the tool lets you add a header image, use a clean photo — the Image Resizer can size it correctly so it does not load slowly or crop badly.

Add a thank-you message at the end. A simple “Thank you — your answer helps us decide what to do next” costs nothing and makes people more willing to answer your next survey too. And always test the survey yourself on your phone before sharing it. If a button is hard to tap or a question looks broken, fix it now.

Share it where your people actually are

A great survey nobody sees is worthless. Share it where the people you want to hear from already spend their time.

For a class, team, or community group, a WhatsApp message with the link works best. Keep the message short: one line about why it matters, the link, and how long it takes. Long explanations get skipped.

For a wider audience, post the link on your social accounts with a short caption. The Caption Generator can help you write a post that sounds like you, and the Hashtag Generator can find a few honest tags so strangers interested in the topic can find it.

For printed places — a shop counter, a classroom wall, a community board — a QR code beats a typed link. The QR Generator turns your survey link into a code people can scan in seconds, and you can print it on a poster or a receipt.

If the link is long and ugly, shorten it first with the Link Shortener so it looks clean in messages and posts, and so people can type it if they need to.

Send one reminder, not five. A single friendly reminder after a day or two doubles responses. Five reminders make people ignore you — and your next survey too.

Read the results without fooling yourself

Answers are only useful if you read them honestly. Start with the simple summary most tools give you: how many people answered, and how the answers split across the options. That is your headline.

Then look for patterns, not single answers. One angry comment is a story; twenty people saying the same thing is a fact. Pay most attention to what repeats.

Watch out for small numbers. If only eight people answered, a “75% said yes” is just six people. Do not turn small samples into big claims. Say how many people answered whenever you share the results.

Read the open-text answers yourself. Skim them all, not just the first few. Sort them into two or three themes — “price”, “delivery”, “taste” — and count how often each theme appears. That simple sorting turns a pile of comments into a decision.

And check who did not answer. If you sent the survey to customers but only happy ones replied, your results are sunnier than reality. That does not make the survey useless — it tells you whose voices are missing, and that is worth knowing before you act.

Privacy: collect less, explain more

People share honest answers when they feel safe. So collect only what you need. If the survey works without names, email addresses, or phone numbers, do not ask for them. Every extra personal question costs you answers and trust.

Say plainly at the top what you collect and why: “We ask for your email only if you want the results — it is optional.” Then keep that promise. Never use survey answers for a purpose you did not mention.

Make sensitive questions optional. Income, health, or personal opinions are questions people may skip, and that is their right. A skipped question is better than a dishonest answer or a person who quits the survey entirely.

If you offer a prize draw to encourage answers, pick the winner fairly with the Random Number Generator instead of choosing by hand — and say so in advance. Transparency about the draw builds the same trust as transparency about the data.

Finally, remember that survey answers belong to the people who gave them. Store them safely, share only summaries, and never publish anyone’s individual answers without their clear permission.

Mistakes that ruin good surveys

Most bad surveys fail for the same few reasons, and all of them are easy to avoid.

The first is asking too many questions. A twenty-question survey about a two-minute topic tells people you do not value their time.

The second is leading questions that push people toward the answer you want. You get flattering numbers and learn nothing.

The third is vague questions. “Do you like our service?” means something different to everyone who reads it. Ask about one specific thing instead.

The fourth is forgetting the mobile phone. Most people will open your survey on a phone. If it is hard to tap, hard to read, or slow to load, they leave.

The fifth is never sharing the results. People gave you their time. Tell them what you learned and what you will change. A survey that disappears into silence teaches people not to answer the next one.

And the sixth is acting on too little data. Eight answers are a hint, not a verdict. If the decision matters, get more answers first.

A simple 7-step survey plan

Put the whole guide together into one plan you can follow for any survey:

  1. Write your goal: One sentence about what you want to learn, and which decision it will change.
  2. Draft eight questions or fewer: Mix multiple choice, one or two ratings, and one open question at the end. Check each one with the Word Counter and the Character Counter to keep them short.
  3. Build it free: Use Google Forms, Microsoft Forms, or Typeform — pick one and keep the design simple.
  4. Test it: Answer it yourself on your phone, then ask two friends to try it. Fix anything confusing.
  5. Share it: Send the link where your people are, shorten it with the Link Shortener, and print a QR code with the QR Generator for offline places.
  6. Remind once: One friendly reminder after a day or two, then stop.
  7. Read and share back: Look for patterns, count the themes in open answers, and tell people what you learned and what you will do next.

Follow these seven steps and a survey stops being guesswork. It becomes a simple habit: ask clearly, listen honestly, and act on what you hear.

Frequently asked questions

Can I create a survey for free without an app?

Yes. Google Forms, Microsoft Forms, and Typeform all offer free plans that run entirely in your browser — no download, no payment. Open one on your phone or computer, add your questions, and share the link.

How many questions should a survey have?

Five to ten questions is the sweet spot for most surveys, taking under three minutes. Fewer questions mean more people finish, and the answers you get are more honest because people are not tired.

What is the difference between a survey and a poll?

A poll asks one quick question with fixed options and gives you a fast winner. A survey asks several questions and tells you the reasons behind the answers. Use a poll for one fast decision and a survey when you need to understand why.

How do I get more people to answer my survey?

Keep it short, say honestly how long it takes, share it where your people already are, and send one friendly reminder. A QR code from the QR Generator helps in printed places, and a clean short link from the Link Shortener looks better in messages.

Should I ask for names or email addresses in my survey?

Only if you truly need them. Anonymous surveys get more honest answers. If you need contact details for a prize draw or follow-up, make that question optional and explain why you are asking.

How do I read open-text answers?

Read them all, then sort them into two or three themes and count how often each theme appears. The themes that repeat are your real findings. Use the Word Counter to check the length of your own questions too — short questions get better answers.
