How to Create Accessible Color Palettes for Your Website (Free Tools Guide)

Color is one of the most powerful design tools on the web — it sets the mood, guides the eye, and makes a brand memorable. But color can also be a barrier. If your text is hard to read against its background, some visitors simply won't be able to use your site: people with low vision, older users, and anyone reading on a phone in bright sunlight. The good news is that making your colors accessible doesn't mean making them boring. An accessible palette is simply a well-thought-out palette — and designing one is a skill anyone can learn with free tools. This guide walks you through it step by step, from picking your first color to checking every combination, using only free tools that run in your browser.

What makes a palette accessible?

At the heart of color accessibility is one simple idea: contrast ratio. This is a number that describes how much a piece of text stands out from the background behind it. It runs from 1:1 (white text on a white background — invisible) to 21:1 (black text on a white background — maximum contrast). The higher the number, the easier the text is to read.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) — the widely-used public standard for web accessibility — recommend two key targets:

  • 4.5:1 for normal body text. This is the level most websites should aim for with paragraphs, form labels, and small UI text.
  • 3:1 for large text (roughly 18pt or 14pt bold and above) and important graphical elements like button borders and icons.

You don't need to memorize these numbers or calculate them by hand. A Contrast Checker does the math for you — enter your two colors and it tells you the ratio and whether it meets the guidelines. With that tool in hand, building an accessible palette becomes a practical, repeatable process. Let's go through it.

Step 1: Pick your base color

Every palette starts with one anchor: your base color. This is usually your brand color — the one people will associate with your site. Choose it by mood rather than by trend. Deep blues and teals feel trustworthy and professional, which is why banks and tech sites love them. Warm oranges and yellows feel energetic and friendly. Greens suggest nature, health, and growth. There's no wrong answer, but there is one rule: pick a color you can build a whole family around.

If you already have a logo, extract your exact brand color instead of guessing. Open our free Color Picker, upload your logo or brand image, and click on the color to get its exact HEX value (for example, a strong brand blue might be #2563EB). Guessing from memory usually lands you a shade that's "close but not quite," and that small mismatch becomes visible when your logo sits next to your buttons. Getting the exact value takes ten seconds and keeps everything consistent.

Step 2: Build the palette

One color isn't a palette. A practical website palette needs five roles filled:

  • A base color — your brand anchor from Step 1. Used for primary buttons, key headings, and brand moments.
  • Two to three shades and tints of the base — darker shades for hover states and footers, lighter tints for backgrounds and highlights. You can derive these from your base HEX by adjusting brightness, which you can do visually in the Color Picker.
  • One accent color — a contrasting color used sparingly for calls to action, alerts, or highlights. It should draw the eye without competing with the base.
  • A neutral dark — for body text. Near-blacks like #1F2937 or #111827 look softer and more modern than pure black, and pair beautifully with white backgrounds.
  • A neutral light — for backgrounds and cards. Off-whites and light grays like #F9FAFB or #F3F4F6 keep pages airy while giving you places to lay content.

If choosing an accent color feels hard, use simple color harmony ideas. An analogous accent sits next to your base color on the color wheel (for example, teal next to blue) — calm and cohesive. A complementary accent sits opposite your base (orange against blue) — bold and attention-grabbing, which makes it great for buttons you want people to click. Either approach works; the accent just needs to feel intentional, not random.

Step 3: Check every text/background pair

This is the most important step, and it's where most websites fail. Open the Contrast Checker and test every combination of text and background your site actually uses: body text on the page background, headings on their sections, button text on button backgrounds, footer text on the footer color, and form labels on input backgrounds.

Aim for the 4.5:1 guideline for all body and small text. For large headings, the 3:1 guideline is the minimum — but hitting 4.5:1 there too is even better and removes all doubt. Pay special attention to buttons: a beautiful blue button with white text is only beautiful if people can read the label. Test the default, hover, and disabled states, because hover colors are often lighter and can slip below the guideline.

Don't forget link colors. Links sitting inside body text need to be distinguishable from the surrounding text as well as readable. A medium blue on white is the classic choice because it passes contrast easily. If you restyle links to match your brand color, run that color through the checker too — brand colors that look great as large logo shapes are sometimes too light for small underlined text.

When a pair fails, you rarely need a whole new color — usually nudging the text darker or the background lighter by a small amount is enough. Small adjustments preserve your design while fixing readability, which is the whole point of an accessible approach.

Step 4: Test gradients and image overlays

Solid colors are easy to check. Gradients are trickier, because the contrast between your text and the background changes along the gradient — it's strong at one end and weak at the other. The rule is simple: your text must pass the contrast check at BOTH ends of the gradient, not just the middle or the darkest part.

Use our free Gradient Generator to build your gradient and preview it live. Take the HEX values at each end and run both through the Contrast Checker against your text color. If either end fails, darken that end of the gradient until it passes. A common fix is to anchor a gradient with a solid dark stop at the end where the text sits.

The same principle applies to text over photos, like hero banners. A photo's brightness varies wildly — white text that reads perfectly over a dark forest can vanish over a bright sky in the same image. The reliable fix is a dark overlay: a semi-transparent dark layer (often black at 40–60% opacity) between the photo and the text. This evens out the contrast across the whole image so your headline stays readable no matter what's behind it.

Step 5: Convert and document your palette

Once your palette passes every check, write it down — future you (and any designer or developer you work with) will thank you. The web's native color language is HEX (like #2563EB), which is what you'll use in CSS. Sometimes you need RGB instead — for example, when adding transparency with rgba() or when a design tool expects it. Our Hex to RGB Converter translates between the two instantly, so you never have to convert by hand.

Here's a small example style reference you can model your own documentation on:

  • Primary (brand blue): #2563EB
  • Primary dark (hover/footer): #1D4ED8
  • Primary light (tint/highlight): #DBEAFE
  • Accent (call to action): #F59E0B
  • Text (neutral dark): #1F2937
  • Background (neutral light): #F9FAFB

Keep this list in your project notes or style guide, and every future button, banner, and page will stay consistent — and consistently readable.

5 common color mistakes to avoid

  • Light gray text on a white background. The classic offender. It looks elegant in a mockup and unreadable in real life, especially on dim phone screens. If your body text is gray, make it dark gray (#374151 or darker).
  • Using red and green as the only way to show meaning. Red/green color blindness is the most common type, so "red means error, green means success" fails for a noticeable share of users. Pair colors with icons, labels, or text — a red ✕ and a green ✓ — so meaning never depends on color alone.
  • Low-contrast placeholder text. Form placeholders are already small and light by convention, which makes them prime suspects. Test them like any other text, or make sure every field also has a real, high-contrast label.
  • Ignoring dark mode. If your site offers a dark theme, your palette needs a second pass: colors that passed on white may fail on dark backgrounds. Check both themes with the Contrast Checker.
  • Too many competing colors. A rainbow of equally loud colors means nothing stands out — and each new color is another set of pairs to verify. One base, one accent, and neutrals are enough for almost any site.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be a designer to make an accessible palette?

No. Accessibility is mostly about contrast, not taste. If you follow the steps above — pick a base, build a small family of colors, and check every text/background pair — you'll end up with a palette that works, even with zero design training.

Does an accessible palette limit my creativity?

Quite the opposite. Constraints breed creativity: brands with the strongest visual identities often use just two or three disciplined colors. Accessibility rules push you toward bolder, clearer choices rather than washed-out ones.

Is the 4.5:1 contrast ratio a legal requirement?

It depends on where you operate and who your audience is. The 4.5:1 and 3:1 figures come from WCAG, the public accessibility standard referenced by many laws and policies worldwide. Regardless of legal specifics, meeting them is simply good practice — it makes your site usable by more people.

Can I check contrast for an existing website?

Yes. Grab the HEX values of your site's text and background colors with the Color Picker (you can sample them from a screenshot), then test each pair in the Contrast Checker. Fix the failures one pair at a time, starting with body text.
