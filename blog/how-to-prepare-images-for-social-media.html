How to Prepare Images for Social Media: Sizes, Formats & Compression (2026 Guide)

You designed a beautiful graphic, uploaded it to Instagram, and it came out blurry — or your website hero image takes five seconds to load and visitors bounce before it appears. Both problems have the same cause: the image was uploaded at the wrong size, in the wrong format, or without compression. Social platforms aggressively re-compress anything you upload, so feeding them a giant file doesn't buy quality — it just slows your upload and lets their algorithm mangle your pixels. This guide gives you the three steps that fix it: the right dimensions, the right format, and the right compression — all free, all in your browser, all private.

Step 1: Use the right dimensions

Every platform crops and scales uploads to its own layouts. Uploading a random size forces the platform to resample your image, which softens edges and text. Match the target dimensions and you keep control of how it looks. The most useful sizes to remember:

  • Instagram square post: 1080 × 1080 px (1:1). Portrait posts perform well at 1080 × 1350 px (4:5).
  • Instagram/Facebook story & reels cover: 1080 × 1920 px (9:16).
  • Facebook feed post / link preview: 1200 × 630 px.
  • X (Twitter) post: 1600 × 900 px (16:9).
  • LinkedIn post: 1200 × 1200 px or 1080 × 1350 px.
  • YouTube thumbnail: 1280 × 720 px (16:9).
  • Website hero / blog cover: 1920 px wide is the practical maximum; anything wider just wastes bandwidth.

Don't eyeball it: open our free Image Resizer, enter the exact pixel dimensions, and lock the aspect ratio so the image can't be stretched. Resizing down from a larger original is always better than scaling up a small image — upscaling invents pixels and looks soft. If your original is smaller than the target size, accept the original's size and let the platform handle it; a sharp small image beats a blurry big one.

Step 2: Pick the right format

Format choice is a trade-off between quality and file size. Here's the plain-English version:

  • WebP: the best all-rounder for web and most social use — smaller files than JPG at the same quality, with transparency support. If a platform accepts it, use it.
  • JPG: the safe default for photos and realistic images. Small files, universal support, but no transparency and quality degrades with each re-save.
  • PNG: use for graphics with sharp edges, text, logos, or transparency (infographics, screenshots, UI mockups). Files are larger than JPG for photos, so don't use PNG for photographs.
  • AVIF: even smaller than WebP with excellent quality, but support is still patchy — check the platform before relying on it.

The practical rule: photos → JPG or WebP; graphics with text or transparency → PNG or WebP. Step 3: Compress before you upload

Compression is where most people leave the biggest gains on the table. A phone photo at full resolution can be 4–8 MB; after smart compression, the visually identical version is 150–300 KB — and it uploads instantly, loads fast on slow connections, and won't get mangled by the platform's own re-compression as badly.

  4. Download the compressed file and use that for your upload.

5 mistakes that ruin social images

  • Uploading huge originals. More pixels don't mean more quality on a phone screen — they mean slower uploads and harsher platform re-compression.
  • Using PNG for photographs. A photo saved as PNG can be 5–10× larger than the same photo as JPG with no visible benefit.
  • Ignoring aspect ratios. A 16:9 banner squeezed into a square slot gets cropped by the platform — and the crop is never where you wanted it. Resize to the slot's ratio first.
  • Adding text to the image at tiny sizes. Text baked into an image re-compresses badly; keep on-image text large (at least 40–50 px tall at 1080 width) or add captions in the platform's own text tools.
  • Uploading images with personal metadata. Photos carry EXIF data — location, device, timestamps. Strip it before uploading anything public (our compressor removes metadata during processing).

Frequently asked questions

What is the best image size for Instagram in 2026?

1080 × 1080 px for square posts, 1080 × 1350 px for portrait posts, and 1080 × 1920 px for stories and reels. These match the platform's display sizes exactly, so no resampling softens your image.

Is WebP better than JPG for social media?

Usually yes — WebP gives similar visual quality at 25–35% smaller file sizes and supports transparency. JPG remains the safest choice where WebP isn't accepted.

How much should I compress before uploading?

Compress until the file is under 200–500 KB (depending on dimensions) while looking identical to the original at full zoom. Quality 80–85 in our Image Compressor is the usual sweet spot for photos.

Will compression hurt my print quality?

Yes — this guide is for screen viewing only. For print, keep the uncompressed originals and use print-specific settings (300 DPI, full quality, CMYK color).
