How to Write Product Descriptions That Sell — with Free Online Tools (2026 Guide)

A good product description does two jobs at once: it answers the buyer's questions before they ask them, and it gives search engines the words people actually type when they shop. A weak description does neither — it lists features, repeats the product name three times, and leaves the buyer wondering about size, materials, delivery and returns. The good news is that writing a strong description is a repeatable process, not a talent. In this guide, you will learn a simple structure that works for online shops, marketplaces and social selling, plus the free tools that make each step faster — all in your browser, with no signup.

Start with the buyer, not the product

Before you write a word, answer four questions about the person who will read it. Who is buying this? What problem does it solve for them? What will they worry about before paying? And what words would they type into a search box to find it? A handmade soap buyer worries about skin type and ingredients; a phone-case buyer worries about fit and drop protection; a dress buyer worries about size, fabric and washing. If your description answers those worries in the first few lines, the buyer keeps reading. If it opens with a history of your brand, they leave.

Write the answers down in plain words first. Those notes become your description — and, usefully, they are also the search terms your listing should contain, because buyers search in the same plain words they think in.

The 5-part structure that works

Use this structure for every listing, and buyers will always find what they need in the same place:

  1. One clear opening line. Say what the product is, who it is for, and its main benefit, in a single sentence. Example: "A soft, washable cotton tote that carries a laptop, a book and a water bottle without straining your shoulder."
  2. Benefits before features. Features describe the product; benefits describe what the buyer gets. "Made of 16 oz canvas" is a feature. "Thick canvas that will not tear under a heavy load" is a benefit. Give the benefit first, then the feature that proves it.
  3. The facts box. Size, weight, materials, colours, what is included, and care instructions. Buyers compare listings on facts, so make them easy to scan — a short list beats a paragraph here.
  4. Answers to the worries. Delivery time, returns, exchanges, and how to choose a size. If you sell clothing, a simple size note ("Model is 5'6" and wears size M") prevents more returns than any other sentence you can write.
  5. A simple call to action. One line at the end: what to do next, and what happens after they order. "Order today and it ships within 2 working days" is clearer than "Buy now!!!" and it sets an expectation you can meet.

Words that help, and words that hurt

Certain habits quietly weaken descriptions. Watch for these in your own drafts:

  • Vague superlatives: "best quality", "amazing", "premium" — every listing says them, so buyers ignore them. Replace each one with a concrete detail: a material, a measurement, or a test result.
  • Keyword stuffing: repeating the product name or a search term five times in one paragraph reads as spam to buyers and to search engines. Use the key term in the title, once in the opening line, and once more naturally later. That is enough.
  • Missing measurements: "large", "small" and "fits most" mean different things to different people. Give numbers: centimetres, grams, capacity, and the exact models a case or cover fits.
  • Hidden costs: if delivery, batteries, or an accessory are not included, say so plainly. A surprise at checkout loses the sale and earns a bad review; honesty up front keeps it.
  • No story at all: a single human sentence — who made it, how it is used, what it replaces — helps a buyer picture the product in their own life. One sentence is enough; a life story is not.

A worked example: before and after

Before: "Beautiful handmade candle. Best quality wax. Amazing smell. Buy now. Premium product for your home decoration."

After: "A hand-poured soy candle with a calm lavender scent, made for quiet evenings at home. It burns for around 40 hours, comes in a reusable glass jar, and is poured in small batches so every candle smells the same. Size: 200 g, jar 8 cm wide. Wick: cotton, lead-free. Ships within 2 working days; returns accepted within 14 days."

The second version is longer, but every extra word answers a question: what it is made of, how long it lasts, what size it is, and what happens after ordering. That is the whole technique — replace claims with answers.

Adapt one description for every platform

You rarely write a description once. The same product may appear on your own shop, a marketplace, and a social media post, each with different length limits. Write the full version first, then adapt it: keep the opening line and facts box everywhere, shorten the middle for marketplaces, and use only the opening line plus a link for social posts. Check each shortened version with the Word Counter and Character Counter before publishing, so a platform limit never cuts your description mid-sentence.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a product description be?

For most products, 150 to 300 words is a good target. Simple products need less; technical or expensive products need more, because buyers have more questions before spending more money. Let the buyer's questions set the length, not a fixed number.

Should I use bullet points or paragraphs?

Both. Use a short paragraph for the opening and the story, and bullet points for facts, sizes and what is included. Buyers scan for facts and read for feeling — give them both formats.

Do keywords really matter in a description?

Yes, but only the words buyers actually search for: the product type, the material, the size, and the use. Write those words naturally into the title and opening line, and do not repeat them unnaturally. A description written for a human buyer almost always contains the right search words anyway.

How often should I update a description?

Whenever the product, price, size range or delivery terms change — and whenever buyers keep asking the same question. A repeated question is a missing sentence in your description. Add it, and the next buyer will not need to ask.

Write for one buyer, answer their worries before they ask, give exact facts, and check the length with free tools before you publish. Do that for every listing, and your descriptions stop being filler around a photo — they become the reason a visitor becomes a customer.
