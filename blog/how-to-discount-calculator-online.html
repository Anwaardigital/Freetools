How to Calculate Discounts Online for Free (2026 Guide)

"35% off!" sounds great — but off what, and what do you actually pay? Sale signs are designed to make the discount feel bigger than it is, and mental math on a 1,899 price tag with 35% off is nobody's idea of fun. A free online discount calculator gives you the two numbers that matter — what you pay and what you save — in one click. This guide shows the steps, works through real examples, and exposes the classic "stacked discount" trick retailers hope you won't notice.

Step 1: Open the Discount Calculator

Open our Discount Calculator. Free, no signup, runs entirely in your browser.

Step 2: Enter price and discount

Two fields:

  • Original price — the pre-sale price (pre-filled with 100)
  • Discount % — the percentage off (pre-filled with 20)

Use the price the discount is applied to — usually the crossed-out "was" price on the tag. If the tag shows the discount as an amount ("save 500") rather than a percent, use our Percentage Calculator (mode "X is what % of Y?") to convert it first.

Step 3: Click "Calculate"

Press "Calculate" and you get:

  • You pay — the final sale price
  • You save — the discount amount, shown in green

Worked example 1: 20% off a 2,500 jacket

  • You save: 2,500 × 20 ÷ 100 = 500
  • You pay: 2,500 − 500 = 2,000

Clean and instant — the exact numbers the tag's "20% OFF" was hiding.

Worked example 2: 35% off an 899 gadget

  • You save: 899 × 35 ÷ 100 = 314.65
  • You pay: 899 − 314.65 = 584.35

This is the case where the calculator earns its keep — 35% of 899 is not a number anyone computes confidently in their head while standing in a store aisle.

The stacked-discount trick (read this)

Here's the honest retail math most shoppers get wrong: "extra 10% off sale prices" does NOT mean add the percentages. If an item is already 30% off and you get another 10% off:

  1. First discount: 1,000 → 700 (30% off)
  2. Second discount applies to 700, not 1,000: 700 → 630 (10% off)

Total discount = 37%, not 40%. To compute it: run the calculator once (1,000 at 30% → 700), then run it again with the result (700 at 10% → 630). Two clicks, and you see through the trick.

Comparing two "deals"

Which is better: 25% off 1,600, or a flat 400 off 1,600? Run both:

  • 25% off: you pay 1,200, save 400
  • Flat 400 off: you pay 1,200, save 400 — identical here

Try 25% off 2,000 vs. flat 400 off 2,000: the percentage wins (pay 1,500 vs. 1,600). The crossover point is exactly where flat-discount ÷ price = percentage — the calculator makes these comparisons take seconds.

When a "discount" isn't actually a deal

Not every sale saves you money, and the calculator can prove it. The classic red flags: a "was" price nobody ever paid (check the item's price history or competitors), "up to 70% off" where only one clearance item hits 70%, and discounts on inflated base prices that land exactly at the normal street price elsewhere. Run the numbers, then do a 30-second search for the same product at two other stores — if their regular price matches the "sale" price, the discount is theater. Also watch bundle deals: "3 for the price of 2" is just 33% off, which you can verify in the calculator in one step. A genuine deal survives comparison; a fake one collapses under it. The two minutes you spend checking routinely saves more than any coupon code.

Smart shopping tips

  • Check the "was" price: inflated original prices make discounts look bigger. Compare with the same item at other stores before celebrating.
  • Stack discounts sequentially in the calculator (above) — never add percentages together.
  • Factor in tax and shipping: a 30% discount evaporates if shipping costs more than you saved. Add those to "you pay" mentally.
  • Ask about the discount base: some coupons apply to the pre-tax total, some to post-tax — it changes the final number slightly.
  • A discount only saves money if you'd buy it anyway. 70% off something you don't need is still 30% wasted.

Frequently asked questions

Is the discount calculator free?

Yes — free, unlimited, no signup, computed in your browser.

Does it work with any currency?

Yes — enter any numbers; the math is currency-agnostic.

How do I handle "buy one get one 50% off"?

Add both items' full prices together as the "original price," then find the effective discount: a 100 + 100 pair where the second is half off = 150 total = 25% off 200. Enter original price 200, discount 25%.

Why does the store's final price differ by a few cents?

Rounding: stores round per-item or per-line, while the tool rounds the final total. Differences are tiny and the tool's figure is the mathematically exact one.
