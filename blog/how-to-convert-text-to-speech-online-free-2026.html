How to Convert Text to Speech Online Free (2026)

Some days your eyes are tired, your hands are busy, or a long article is simply easier to hear than to read. Text to speech turns written words into spoken audio — and you can do it completely free, in your browser, with no app to install and no account to create. This guide shows you how to convert any text to speech online, how to pick a voice and speed that sound natural, and how to use the audio for studying, proofreading, learning a language, or simply getting through a long read while you walk, cook, or commute.

Why convert text to speech?

  • Listen instead of reading: Turn long articles, study notes, reports and emails into audio you can play while doing something else.
  • Rest your eyes: After hours on a screen, listening is a genuine break — and you still get through the material.
  • Catch your own mistakes: Hearing your writing read aloud is the fastest way to find awkward sentences, missing words and repeated phrases. Writers and students swear by it.
  • Accessibility: For anyone with low vision, dyslexia, or reading difficulties, speech is not a convenience — it is how the text becomes usable.
  • Language learning: Hearing correct pronunciation while you read helps new words stick, in your own language or a foreign one.
  • Proofreading on the move: Listen to a draft on your phone while walking, and note the spots that sound wrong.

Method 1: Use the free Text to Speech tool

The simplest way is our Text to Speech tool, which runs entirely in your browser — your text is never uploaded to a server:

  1. Open the Text to Speech tool.
  2. Paste or type your text into the box. You can also use the Word Counter first to check the length — a good rule is that one minute of speech is roughly 130 to 150 words.
  3. Pick a voice from the list. Your browser offers the voices installed on your device, usually in several languages.
  4. Set the speed. Start at normal speed, then slow down for study material or speed up for a quick listen-through.
  5. Press play. Pause, skip, or replay any part as you like.

Because the tool uses your browser's built-in speech, it works on Android, iPhone, Windows and Mac, and the audio quality depends on the voices your device already has — most modern phones and computers include several clear, natural-sounding voices for free.

Choosing the right voice and speed

A good listening experience is mostly about two settings: voice and speed. Try a few voices before settling — a voice that sounds fine for a sentence can become tiring over ten minutes. For study material, choose a clear, steady voice at slightly slower than normal speed; for a quick review of something you already know, a faster speed saves time. If you are learning a language, pick a voice in that language and listen at normal speed first, then slow down to catch the pronunciation of difficult words.

One practical tip: different browsers offer different voices. If the voices on your phone sound robotic, try the same page in another browser on the same device — Chrome, Edge and Firefox each bring their own set, and one of them usually has a noticeably better voice for your language.

Best uses for text to speech

Studying and revision

Paste your notes into the tool and listen to them while walking or doing chores. Hearing the material a second time, in a different form, helps it stick — and you can replay the tricky sections as often as you like. Short chunks work best: split long notes into sections of a few hundred words each, and listen to one section at a time.

Proofreading your own writing

Before you send an important email, submit an assignment, or publish a post, paste the text into the Text to Speech tool and listen to the whole thing. Errors your eyes skipped — a missing word, a doubled word, a sentence that runs on too long — jump out when you hear them. If a sentence sounds confusing when spoken, rewrite it until it sounds clear.

Reading long articles hands-free

Copy a long article into the tool and listen while you commute, exercise, or cook. To get the length right first, paste the text into the Word Counter or the Character Counter — a 1,500-word article is about ten minutes of audio.

Language practice

Type or paste a sentence in the language you are learning, choose a voice in that language, and listen carefully. Then repeat the sentence out loud yourself, matching the rhythm. It is a free pronunciation coach that never gets tired.

Tips for natural-sounding results

  • Use punctuation properly: The speech engine pauses at commas and full stops, so punctuated text sounds far more natural than a wall of unbroken words. Add a comma where you would pause when speaking.
  • Keep sentences short: Long, winding sentences confuse the rhythm. Break them into shorter ones and the audio instantly sounds better.
  • Spell out numbers and abbreviations: "Dr." and "e.g." can be read oddly. Writing "Doctor" or "for example" gives a cleaner result.
  • Split very long texts: A 10,000-word document in one go can stutter or stop on some devices. Split it into parts — the Word Counter helps you find natural break points.
  • Remove what should not be spoken: Strip out URLs, navigation menus, and stray symbols before pasting. A clean text gives a clean listen.
  • Trim the audio later if needed: If you record the speech, our Audio Trimmer cuts out the beginning, the end, or mistakes in the middle.

Privacy: why browser-based conversion wins

Many "free text to speech" sites upload your text to their servers — your study notes, your draft, your private email — where it may be stored or used to train their systems. The tool in this guide works differently: the speech is generated by your own browser, on your own device, and your text never leaves your computer or phone. That matters most for sensitive material: a job application, a medical note, a business document, or anything you would not want sitting on a stranger's server. Free should not mean giving your words away.

Text to speech and speech to text, together

The two tools are a pair. Text to Speech turns your writing into audio; Speech to Text turns your spoken words into written text. Together they form a simple loop: dictate a first draft with Speech to Text, clean it up, then listen to it with Text to Speech to check how it sounds. Writers use this loop to draft faster, students use it to turn spoken notes into written revision material, and anyone with a busy day can move between speaking, writing and listening without ever opening a paid app.

Frequently asked questions

Is text to speech really free?

Yes — the tool in this guide is completely free, with no signup, no limits and no watermarks. It uses the speech voices already built into your browser and device.

Can I download the audio as an MP3?

The built-in browser voices play directly and cannot always be saved as a file. If you need a saved audio file, record the playback with your device's screen recorder or a free audio recorder, then use the Audio Trimmer to cut it to size.

Which languages are supported?

It depends on the voices installed on your device. Most phones and computers include voices for English, Urdu, Hindi, Arabic, Turkish and many other languages. If your language is missing, check your device settings — most systems let you download extra voices for free.

Is there a text length limit?

There is no strict limit in the tool, but very long texts can be slow or stop partway on some browsers. For the best results, keep each listen under a few thousand words — roughly twenty minutes of audio — and split longer documents into parts.

Can I use it for my YouTube videos or podcasts?

For personal listening and study, yes. For published videos, check the licence of the specific voice you use, because some system voices are licensed for personal use only. If you publish audio, record your own voice or use a voice with a clear commercial licence.
