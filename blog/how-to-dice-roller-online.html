How to Roll Dice Online for Free (2026 Guide)

The dice rolled under the sofa again. Or you're playing a game that needs a 20-sided die you don't own. An online dice roller solves both problems: it simulates any number of dice with any number of sides, instantly, on any device. This guide shows you how to roll dice online for free, step by step — from a basic board-game roll to the exotic dice of tabletop RPGs.

Step-by-step: roll dice with our free tool

Our Dice Roller runs entirely in your browser — no signup, no app install. Here's how to use it:

  1. Open the Dice Roller. You'll see two settings: "Dice" (how many dice to roll) and "Sides" (how many sides each die has).
  2. Set the number of dice — anything from 1 to 10. Playing a game that needs two dice? Enter 2.
  3. Set the number of sides — anything from 2 to 100. Standard board games use 6; RPGs use 4, 8, 10, 12, 20 or even 100.
  4. Click "🎲 Roll". The result shows each die's value and the total, e.g. "🎲 4 • 6 = 10".
  5. Roll again as many times as you need — it's unlimited.

Dice notation: reading "2d6" and "1d20"

Gamers write dice rolls in a shorthand you'll see in rulebooks: NdS means "roll N dice with S sides each." So:

  • 1d6 = one 6-sided die (set Dice: 1, Sides: 6)
  • 2d6 = two 6-sided dice, added together (set Dice: 2, Sides: 6)
  • 1d20 = one 20-sided die (set Dice: 1, Sides: 20)
  • 1d100 = percentile roll, 1–100 (set Dice: 1, Sides: 100)

Our tool shows both the individual dice and their sum, which covers the vast majority of game rules.

Real use cases

  • Board games with lost dice: The classic emergency. Set 1d6 (or 2d6 for games like Monopoly or Backgammon) and play on.
  • Tabletop RPGs (D&D, Pathfinder): Roll 1d20 for attacks and skill checks, 1d8 or 1d12 for damage, 1d100 for percentile tables — no physical dice set required.
  • Party and drinking games: Roll on a shared screen so everyone sees the result at once.
  • Classroom probability: Roll 2d6 thirty times and chart the totals — students discover the bell curve themselves (7 is the most common total).
  • Decision making: Choosing among up to 100 options? Number them, set Sides to the count, roll 1 die. More flexible than a coin flip when you have more than two choices.
  • Remote / video-call games: Share your screen and roll for the whole group — everyone trusts what they can see.

Practical example: a board game night

You're playing a game that needs 2d6 for movement. Open the roller, set Dice: 2, Sides: 6, and click Roll. The display shows something like "🎲 3 • 5 = 8" — move 8 spaces. Next player's turn? Click Roll again. It takes about two seconds per roll, and there's no die to chase across the room.

Practical example: an RPG attack roll

Your character swings a sword: the rules say roll 1d20 to hit, then 1d8 for damage. First set Dice: 1, Sides: 20 and roll — say you get 17, a hit. Then change Sides to 8, roll again — 6 damage. Two clicks, no dice bag needed.

How fair are virtual dice?

An honest note: the tool uses your browser's standard pseudo-random generator, giving each face an equal probability — a fair die for games, parties and teaching. Physical dice can actually be less fair (worn edges, imperfect balance, sloppy throws). The one thing virtual dice aren't for: real-money gambling or anything requiring certified randomness — regulated games need audited random number generators, which a free web tool doesn't claim to be.

Tips for smooth play

  • Set it once per game phase. If a game uses 2d6 all evening, leave the settings alone and just hit Roll each turn.
  • Read the total, but check the dice. Some rules care about doubles (two matching dice) — the display shows individual values for exactly this reason.
  • For percentile (1d100): set Sides to 100. A result of 1–100 maps directly to percentage tables in many RPGs.
  • Combine with the Random Number Generator when you need many results at once (up to 1,000) or unique numbers with no repeats.
