How to Convert Images to PDF Online for Free (2026 Guide)

Homework photographed page by page. A rental contract scanned at the shop. Receipts for your tax return. Product photos for a client. Again and again, life hands you images — and what you actually need is a single, clean, shareable PDF. Converting images to PDF used to mean installing software or uploading sensitive documents to a stranger's server. In this guide, you'll learn how to do it in your browser, for free, with your files never leaving your device.

Why convert images to PDF?

Images and PDFs serve different jobs. An image is a picture; a PDF is a document. Converting pays off whenever:

  • You're submitting documents: universities, employers and government offices ask for PDFs — one file per document, in order, not twelve loose JPEGs in a chat thread.
  • You're emailing or archiving: a multi-page PDF keeps pages in order, prints consistently on any device, and looks professional.
  • You're sharing contracts or forms: a PDF is far harder to casually edit than a photo, so the document people see is the document you made.
  • You're printing: PDF pages have fixed sizes and margins; images printed directly often crop, stretch or tile unpredictably.

Multiple images: one PDF, in the right order

The most common use case is combining several images into one PDF — a scanned multi-page contract, a photographed notebook, an ID's front and back. The key step is ordering. Add images in sequence and review the thumbnail order before converting; renaming files (page-01, page-02, …) before upload also helps because most tools list files alphabetically.

Mixing orientations is fine — portrait photos and landscape scans can live in one PDF — but for readability keep a consistent orientation within each document when you can. If you already have separate PDFs (say, one PDF per page from a scanner app), combine them afterwards with our free PDF Merger instead.

One image per page vs. multiple images per page

Which layout you choose depends on what the document is for:

  • One image per page is right for scans, contracts, certificates and anything people must read — every page keeps its full size and legibility.
  • Multiple images per page suits photo collections, evidence logs and product catalogs — a contact-sheet style PDF that is compact and easy to browse.
  • Fit vs. fill: "fit" scales the image to the page without cropping (safest for documents); "fill" crops edges to eliminate white margins (better for photos). For anything official, always use fit.

Keep the file size under control

Phone photos are huge — a single 12 MP photo can be 4–6 MB, so a 30-page scan set can easily balloon past 150 MB. That fails email attachment limits and uploads slowly. Fix it before converting:

  • Compress first: run your photos through our Image Compressor to shrink each image with minimal visible quality loss, then convert the compressed images to PDF.
  • Pick sane quality settings: for text documents, medium quality is plenty — high-resolution photos add megabytes without adding readability. Reserve maximum quality for photos people will zoom into.
  • Resize oversized images: a 4000-pixel-wide photo contains far more detail than an A4 page can show. Resizing to around 1500–2000 pixels wide before conversion cuts size dramatically.
  • Compress afterwards too: if the PDF is still too large, our PDF Compressor squeezes it further — useful when a portal caps uploads at 5 or 10 MB.

As a rule of thumb: text documents should land around 100–300 KB per page; a 20-page scanned contract should end up under 5 MB.

Our converter is different by design: everything happens locally in your browser. Your images never leave your device — the conversion runs in JavaScript on your own phone or computer. There is nothing to leak, breach or retain because there is no server receiving your files at all. For documents with personal data, this is the only approach we recommend.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Wrong page order: always preview thumbnails before converting. Reordering after download means starting over.
  • Blurry scans: no converter fixes blur. Photograph documents in bright, even light, hold the phone parallel to the page, and keep fingers out of frame.
  • Sending editable photos instead: if you need the file to be tamper-evident, PDF is the right container — don't send the raw JPEGs.
  • Forgetting the file size: check the downloaded PDF's size before emailing. If it exceeds the recipient's limit, compress it first.
  • Cropping documents: "fill" modes crop image edges — on a contract, that can cut off signatures and margins. Use "fit" for documents.
