How to Look Up an IP Address Online for Free (2026 Guide)

Every device on the internet has an IP address, and every IP address leaves a small trail of public information: roughly where it is, which internet provider operates it, and what timezone it's in. Whether you're checking that your VPN is actually working, troubleshooting why a site shows you the wrong region, or just curious what your own connection reveals to the world, an IP lookup answers in seconds. This guide walks you through our free IP Lookup tool step by step — and explains, honestly, what IP geolocation can and cannot tell you.

What an IP lookup shows (and doesn't)

Enter an IP address or domain into the tool and you'll get back a small dossier: country, region, city, ISP (internet service provider), and timezone. What you will not get is a street address, a person's name, or a precise location. IP geolocation maps an address to the approximate location of the provider's equipment — usually accurate to the city, sometimes only to the region — and that's a hard limit of the technology, not of this particular tool.

Step 1: Enter an IP address or a domain

Open the IP Lookup tool and type into the input box. You can enter either:

  • An IPv4 address — four number groups like 8.8.8.8 (that's Google's public DNS, a handy example to try).
  • A domain name — like example.com. The tool resolves it to its IP address first, then looks that up.

Then click Look Up. Results typically appear within a second or two.

Step 2: Or click "My IP" to check your own connection

Don't have an address handy? Click the My IP button and the tool detects your own public IP address automatically, then runs the lookup on it. This is the fastest way to answer "what does the internet see when I connect?" — useful before and after turning on a VPN to confirm your visible location actually changed.

Step 3: Read the results

Each result field tells you something different:

  • Country / region / city — the approximate location of the IP's network equipment. Treat the city as "probably nearby," not "exactly here."
  • ISP — the organization the address block is registered to, often your internet provider or a hosting company.
  • Timezone — derived from the location; handy for scheduling across regions.

If the ISP shows a hosting company or VPN provider instead of a residential ISP, that's a clue the address belongs to a server or VPN exit node rather than someone's home connection.

Understanding accuracy: an honest picture

IP geolocation databases are built from ISP registrations, network measurements, and user corrections — and they're approximate by nature. Expect these quirks:

  • City-level is the ceiling. Results are usually right about the city or region, but they can be off — especially for mobile networks, where thousands of users may share addresses registered in one city while physically elsewhere.
  • VPNs and proxies relocate you. A lookup shows where the traffic exits to the internet, not where you sit. That's exactly why the tool is useful for verifying a VPN.
  • Corporate and mobile networks wander. A company with offices nationwide may show all employees at headquarters; a phone on cellular data may geolocate to a distant city.
  • Databases go stale. IP blocks get reassigned, and databases take time to catch up — an occasional odd result is normal.

Legitimate uses for IP lookup

  • Verify your VPN: check your IP before and after connecting — the country and ISP should change.
  • Troubleshoot regional issues: if a site serves you the wrong language, currency, or content library, your IP's geolocation is the usual suspect.
  • See your own exposure: whatever the tool shows about you is visible to every website you visit. It's a good reminder of why privacy tools matter.
  • Investigate suspicious senders: an email claiming to be from your bank but sent from an IP geolocated to an unexpected country deserves skepticism (view the message's full headers to find the sending IP).
  • Developer debugging: confirm which server or CDN edge a domain currently resolves to.

A note on privacy: how this tool works

Unlike most tools on this site, which run entirely in your browser, an IP lookup inherently needs outside data — so this tool queries a public geolocation API with the address you enter. That's normal for this kind of tool, but worth knowing: the API provider sees the lookup request. Avoid looking up addresses tied to sensitive investigations from networks you'd rather not associate with the query, and never expect IP data to identify a specific person — it can't, and shouldn't be used to try.

Frequently asked questions

Can IP lookup find someone's exact address?

No. It shows the approximate location of network equipment — city or region level at best. Precise subscriber addresses are held by ISPs and aren't public.

Why does the location show the wrong city for my own IP?

Common with mobile data and some ISPs: your traffic exits through equipment registered elsewhere. It can also mean your VPN is on when you expected it off (or vice versa) — worth checking.

Can I look up a website by its domain name?

Yes — enter the domain and the tool resolves it to an IP first. Note that large sites use many servers worldwide, so you'll see just one of their addresses.

Is it legal to look up an IP address?

Looking up the public registration and geolocation data of an IP address is a routine, legitimate network-diagnostic activity. What's not okay is using it to harass, stalk, or target anyone.
