How do I calculate a discount price?

Choose the decrease mode, enter the original price and the discount percent. The tool shows the discount amount and the final price. Example: 25% off a $80 item = $20 off, final price $60.

How do I find what percent one number is of another?

Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. For example, 45 out of 60 is (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%. Use the percentage-change mode to do this automatically.

How do I calculate a salary raise?

Use the increase mode: enter your current salary and the raise percent. A 10% raise on $50,000 gives $5,000 more, for a new salary of $55,000.

Why do successive discounts not add up?

Because each discount applies to a shrinking base. A 20% discount followed by 10% off is not 30% off — it's 1 − (0.80 × 0.90) = 28% off. Our calculator handles each step so you see the true final price.

Percentage Calculator — Complete Guide

Percentages show up everywhere: sale tags, exam scores, tax rates, tips, interest, and salary negotiations. A Percentage Calculator removes the mental arithmetic so you get exact answers in seconds — and understanding the few core formulas makes you faster even without the tool.

The three core formulas

  • X% of Y: (X ÷ 100) × Y. Example: 15% of 200 = 0.15 × 200 = 30.
  • What percent is A of B: (A ÷ B) × 100. Example: 30 is what percent of 200? (30 ÷ 200) × 100 = 15%.
  • Percentage change: ((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100. A price rising from $50 to $65 is a 30% increase.

Everyday use cases

  • Shopping: verify that the "70% off" tag really gives the advertised price — retailers sometimes inflate the original.
  • Grades: convert raw marks to percentages and work out what you need on the final to hit your target.
  • Finance: compare interest rates, compute tax, and sanity-check investment returns.
  • Business: calculate margins, markups, and year-over-year growth for reports.

Common traps

  • Percentage points vs. percent: a rate rising from 5% to 7% is a 2-percentage-point rise, but a 40% relative increase. Politicians and advertisers mix these up — now you won't.
  • Compounding: a 10% gain followed by a 10% loss doesn't return you to start: $100 → $110 → $99. Losses hurt more than equal gains help.
  • Base confusion: "50% more" and "50% off" are not symmetric. Always ask: percent of what?

Pro tips

For quick mental math, remember 10% is just moving the decimal point one place: 10% of 240 is 24, so 5% is 12 and 15% is 36. For tips, compute 10% and double it for 20%. And when a store stacks coupons, multiply the remaining fractions (0.8 × 0.9) rather than adding the percents.

Reverse percentages

Sometimes you know the result and need the original. If a $68 bill includes 15% tax, the pre-tax price is 68 ÷ 1.15 = $59.13. If your investment grew 40% to $7,000, it started at 7,000 ÷ 1.40 = $5,000. Dividing by (1 + rate) reverses any percentage increase; dividing by (1 − rate) reverses a decrease.
