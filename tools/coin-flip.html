Flip a virtual coin — heads or tails — with live running stats. Useful for settling small decisions and games. Free, unlimited, private — works entirely in your browser.

How to use

  1. Click the 'Flip Coin' button — or just press the spacebar — to flip.
  2. Watch the animated coin spin and land on heads or tails.

FAQ

Is it really free?

Yes — every tool on this site is 100% free with no limits, no signup and no watermarks.

Is my data private?

Absolutely. All processing happens locally in your browser. Your files are never uploaded to any server.

Is the coin flip truly random?

It uses your browser's cryptographically secure random number generator (crypto.getRandomValues), the same source of randomness used for security keys — far better than a basic Math.random() and effectively unpredictable.

Can I use this to make decisions fairly?

Absolutely — that is what it is for. Settling debates, picking who goes first, choosing between two restaurants: a fair coin has no memory and no bias, which makes it the simplest impartial judge.

Do the odds change after several heads in a row?

No — this is the gambler's fallacy. Each flip is independent with a 50/50 chance. Five heads in a row does not make tails 'due'; the coin has no memory of past flips.

Why do the percentages drift from 50/50?

Short runs vary a lot — 7 heads out of 10 flips is completely normal. The percentages converge toward 50/50 only over hundreds or thousands of flips (the law of large numbers). The tally panel lets you watch this happen live.

📘 The Humble Coin Flip: Fairness, Probability & Fun

Few tools are simpler than a coin flip, and few are more useful for settling life's small disputes. But behind the simplicity sits genuinely interesting mathematics — and a few famous misconceptions. This guide covers fair decision-making, the probability behind streaks, and creative ways to use a virtual coin.

Why coin flips are fair

A fair coin has exactly two properties that matter: each side has equal probability (1/2), and each flip is independent of the last. That combination makes it the gold standard of impartiality — no arguments about technique, no "best of three" renegotiations. Courts have literally used coin flips to settle legal ties, from sports drafts to (in at least one documented case) a tied election. When both parties agree to the flip beforehand, the result carries a moral weight that arguing never achieves.

The gambler's fallacy

The most famous coin-flip trap: after four heads, most people feel tails is "due." It is not. The coin does not remember. The probability of heads on the next flip is still exactly 50% — always. Casinos profit enormously from this intuition failure (watch a roulette board's history display sometime). What is true: the probability of five heads in a row from the start is 1 in 32 — but once four heads have already happened, that history is sunk and the next flip stands alone.

Streaks are normal

In 100 flips, a run of 6+ heads or tails in a row is not just possible but expected — it would be suspicious if it never happened. Humans are terrible at intuiting randomness: when asked to invent a "random" sequence, people alternate too neatly (HTHTHT), while real randomness clumps. If your results look "streaky," that is evidence the flipper is working correctly, not broken.

Creative uses

Beyond decisions: use flips to randomise turn order in board games, assign chores, break ties in voting, generate binary sequences for simple experiments, or teach kids probability — have them predict, flip 50 times, and compare the tally to their gut feeling. Teachers: the live percentage display is a perfect classroom demo of the law of large numbers.

How this flipper works

Each flip draws from crypto.getRandomValues(), your browser's cryptographically secure RNG — the same entropy source behind HTTPS keys and password generators. The animation is just theatre; the outcome is decided by real randomness the moment you click. No servers, no tracking, no rigging — refresh the page and the history clears completely.
