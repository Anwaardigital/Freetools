How to use

  1. Enter the date and time you want to convert, or click Now to use the current time.
  2. Select the source timezone from the dropdown (for example America/New_York).
  3. Select one or more target timezones (for example Asia/Karachi) to compare side by side.
  4. Click Convert — each zone shows the equivalent local time with its UTC offset and DST status.

FAQ

Is it really free?

Yes — every tool on this site is 100% free with no limits, no signup and no watermarks.

Is my data private?

Absolutely. All processing happens locally in your browser. Your files are never uploaded to any server.

Does the converter handle daylight saving time automatically?

Yes. It uses the IANA timezone database, so daylight saving transitions are applied correctly for the exact date you enter — including historical changes and announced future rule updates.

How many timezones are supported?

All 400+ IANA timezones, from Pacific/Kiritimati (UTC+14) to Pacific/Midway (UTC−11) — including half-hour zones like Asia/Kolkata and 45-minute zones like Asia/Kathmandu.

Why does a recurring meeting shift for some attendees twice a year?

Because regions change clocks on different schedules: the US and Europe spring forward and fall back on different weekends, and many countries never observe DST at all. Always verify the UTC offset for the specific date, not just the city name.

Can I convert past or future dates, not just today?

Yes. Enter any date and the converter applies the timezone rules in effect on that date, based on the IANA database — useful for checking old logs or planning far-ahead events.

Complete Guide to Timezones

Timezones exist because the Earth rotates: noon in Karachi is morning in London and the previous evening in New York. To keep civil time roughly aligned with the sun, the world is divided into zones measured as offsets from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). Our converter translates any moment between any of the 400+ IANA timezones instantly.

UTC is the anchor. Every timezone is defined as UTC plus or minus some offset, and that offset can change during the year where daylight saving time (DST) is observed. This is why "3 PM in New York" is ambiguous without a date: it is UTC−5 in winter (EST) but UTC−4 in summer (EDT). Storing and transmitting times in UTC, converting only for display, is the golden rule that prevents these bugs.

Real-world uses

  • International meetings: find a slot that is humane for everyone — 9 AM in San Francisco is 9 PM in Karachi, so somebody is always compromising.
  • Travel planning: convert landing times to your home zone to beat jet lag confusion, and check whether your destination observes DST.
  • Broadcasts and events: announce webinars and match times with a timezone converter link so nobody misses out.
  • Log forensics: correlate server logs written in UTC with user reports given in local time.

Common pitfalls

The fall-back hour happens twice: when clocks go back, 1:30 AM occurs twice in one night — always clarify which 1:30 AM you mean. Abbreviations lie: "CST" can mean US Central, China, or Cuba Standard Time — prefer IANA names like America/Chicago. And remember that several regions (most of Asia and Africa) skip DST entirely, so their offset never changes.

Pro tip: when scheduling across zones, propose times in UTC ("1400 UTC") alongside local equivalents. It removes all ambiguity and is standard practice in aviation, tech, and international business.
