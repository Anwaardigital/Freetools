How to use

  1. Paste the suspicious URL into the box — include the full address, starting with http.
  2. Click Check URL to run the heuristic analysis in your browser.

What tricks do phishing links use?

Common tricks include lookalike characters (paypa1.com), extra subdomains (secure.paypal.evil.com), URL shorteners hiding the destination, IP addresses instead of domain names, and urgent language pressuring you to act fast.

Can I check shortened URLs like bit.ly links?

Yes — paste the shortened link and the tool will flag the hidden destination as a risk factor. For extra safety, use a URL expander to reveal the real address before deciding to visit it.

What should I do if a link is flagged as risky?

Don't click it. If it claims to be from your bank or a service you use, open that service's official site or app yourself by typing the address manually. Report phishing emails to your email provider and, for financial scams, to your local cybercrime authority.

Phishing URL Checker — Complete Guide

Phishing is the most common way accounts get stolen: a fake login page that looks exactly like your bank, email, or social media. A Phishing URL Checker gives you a fast first opinion on any suspicious link before you risk clicking it.

How the check works

The tool runs a set of heuristic tests entirely in your browser — nothing is sent anywhere. It looks for red flags that real phishers rely on: IP addresses used instead of domain names, lookalike Unicode characters, excessive subdomains, suspicious top-level domains, mismatched brand names, URL shorteners, and dangerous patterns like "@" tricks or punycode. Each failed check raises the risk score.

Reading the result

  • Low risk: no obvious tricks found — still stay alert, since new phishing domains appear every hour.
  • Medium risk: one or two suspicious traits. Treat the link as guilty until proven innocent.
  • High risk: multiple phishing hallmarks. Do not visit, do not enter any credentials.

Real-world scenarios

  • "Your account will be suspended" emails: paste the link before clicking. Legitimate companies never ask you to log in from an email link under threat.
  • Package delivery SMS: scammers impersonate couriers with "pay a small fee" links. Check the URL, then track your parcel on the courier's real site.
  • Social media DMs: "Is this you in this video?" links from friends whose accounts were hijacked. Verify with the friend first.

Limitations

Heuristics can't catch everything — a freshly registered clean-looking domain can still host a phishing page, and a legitimate site can trip a heuristic. This tool is a safety net, not a verdict. Your best defenses remain: type addresses manually, use a password manager (it won't autofill on fake domains), and enable two-factor authentication everywhere.

Why phishing works

Phishing exploits trust and urgency, not technical brilliance. Attackers impersonate brands you already trust, manufacture deadlines ("verify within 24 hours"), and rely on the fact that most people skim links instead of reading them. Knowing the playbook — authority, scarcity, fear — makes the tricks visible before you click.

Beyond the link check

Pair this tool with good habits: hover to preview links on desktop, long-press on mobile, and check the sender's actual address — not just the display name. Keep your browser updated, since modern browsers block many known phishing domains automatically.
